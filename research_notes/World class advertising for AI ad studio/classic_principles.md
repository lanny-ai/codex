# Classic Advertising Principles and What Modern Evidence Supports

Scope note on method: archive.org and gutenberg.org were unreachable from this research environment (DNS and proxy blocks), so primary book scans could not be read directly. Quotes below come from secondary sources that reproduce them; each is labelled with how well it is verified. Treat any quote marked "secondary only" as needing a check against the printed book before it goes into a public-facing rulebook. Where I recall a well-known fact from training but could not confirm it in this session, it is listed under Gaps, not Findings.

## 1. Claude Hopkins (Scientific Advertising, 1923): specificity, reason-why, testing, samples. Which rules hold up?

### Takeaway
Hopkins's core rules (be specific, sell rather than entertain, key every ad and test cheaply before scaling) are the best-supported part of the classic canon: they are close to how modern paid social and A/B testing actually work. His rules came from coupon-keyed mail-order print in the 1900s to 1920s, so they measure direct response only and say nothing about long-term brand effects.

### Cited Findings
- Hopkins on specificity: "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck. They leave no impression whatever." Several reviews attribute it to *Scientific Advertising* (1923), p. 33 in the edition they used. Secondary only; the page number varies by edition. — [Glide Design review](https://www.glidedesign.com/blog/book-review-scientific-advertising-enlightens/); [Antoine Buteau, Lessons from Claude Hopkins](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-claude-hopkins/); [quote aggregator, Quotefancy](https://quotefancy.com/claude-c-hopkins-quotes)
- The specificity passage goes on to argue that specific figures are not generally discounted by readers, while superlatives are. Paraphrased in secondary sources. — [Antoine Buteau](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-claude-hopkins/); [The Agency Review summary](https://the-agency-review.com/scientific-advertising/)
- Ogilvy endorsed Hopkins as foundational, which is how much of Hopkins's influence reached later practice. — [Drayton Bird, "Ogilvy on Hopkins"](https://draytonbird.com/?p=2365)
- Modern test evidence that headline wording alone moves response: Robertson et al. (2023, *Nature Human Behaviour*) analysed about 105,000 Upworthy headline variants (around 370M impressions and 5.7M clicks) from randomized tests of different headlines for the same story. Word choice changed click-through measurably. This supports Hopkins's claim that copy variants can be tested cheaply and that the differences are real. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10202797/)
- Size of the effect in that dataset: each additional negative word raised CTR by about 2.3% relative. Phys.org reports one mild negative word moving average CTR from about 1.4% to 2.3%, a much larger effect. The sources disagree, so use the paper's own number. — [PsyPost](https://psypost.org/a-particular-type-of-language-boosts-online-news-consumption-study-suggests); [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2023-03-headlines-negative-boost-consumption-online.html); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10202797/)

### Inferences
- Rule for the system: replace every adjective claim with a number, a named mechanism, a timeframe or a named client result, e.g. "booked 14 consults in 30 days" rather than "get more clients". This is Hopkins's specificity rule, and it costs nothing to apply.
- "Samples" maps directly onto a service business's free audit, free strategy call, free checklist or lead magnet. Hopkins's logic (let the product sell itself and capture the name) is the logic of the modern lead-gen ad.
- "Keyed returns" become UTM-tagged variants and platform A/B tests. Hopkins's insistence that one ad's results be separable from another's is the precondition for any learning loop in an AI ad system.
- Weakness: Hopkins measured only immediate, attributable response. That systematically undervalues brand effects (see Section 8). For small service businesses with tiny budgets and a need for cash flow, this bias is mostly acceptable, but it should not be absolute.

### Gaps
- Exact wording and chapter for these Hopkins lines could not be checked against a primary scan: "The only purpose of advertising is to make sales"; "Almost any question can be answered cheaply, quickly and finally by a test campaign"; "Ads are not written to interest, please or amuse you"; and his chapters on samples, headlines and "Offer Service". The book is public domain (1923). Verify against Project Gutenberg or archive.org before quoting.
- I found no modern experimental study that tests a specific claim against a vague claim directly in ads. Research on concreteness and precise numbers exists in consumer psychology (e.g. Packard and Berger on concrete language; work on precise vs round numbers), but I did not retrieve or verify it in this session.

## 2. David Ogilvy (Confessions of an Advertising Man, 1963; Ogilvy on Advertising, 1983): headlines and the "80%" claim, research, "the consumer isn't a moron", long copy, brand image, rules for TV and print. What did later testing confirm or overturn?

### Takeaway
Ogilvy's headline primacy claim ("five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy", hence "eighty cents out of your dollar") is the origin of the folk "80% read only the headline" figure. It is directionally confirmed by modern headline A/B tests and by attention research, but the 5:1 ratio is a mid-century print readership estimate, not a law. His "long copy sells more than short" claim is conditional, and his own wording says "for a great many products", not all.

### Cited Findings
- The headline claim (*Confessions*, 1963), commonly given as: "On the average, five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. When you have written your headline, you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar." Secondary sources agree on this wording; some variants are paraphrases. — [Swipefile](https://swipefile.com/five-times-as-many-people-read-the-headlines-david-ogilvy); [Quartz on Confessions](https://qz.com/1561802); [ReferralCandy](https://referralcandy.com/blog/david-ogilvy-ads)
- The same passage calls the headline the "telegram which decides the reader to go on reading". Secondary only. — [SciMark, "The Art of the Headline"](https://scimark.substack.com/p/the-art-of-the-headline-some-tips)
- On respecting the audience: "The consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife. You insult her intelligence if you assume that a mere slogan and a few tired adjectives will persuade her to buy anything." Attributed to *Confessions* (1963). — [Lessons from David Ogilvy](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-david-ogilvy/); [Entrepreneur, "Master of the Soft Sell"](https://entrepreneur.com/article/197680)
- On long copy: "All my experience says that for a great many products, long copy sells more than short." Another quote attributed to him: "in split-run tests, long copy invariably outsells short copy." Usually sourced to *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983). Secondary only. Ogilvy grounded this in direct-response advertisers, who "know to a dollar how much each advertisement sells". — [Entrepreneur, "5 Laws of Advertising"](https://www.entrepreneur.com/growing-a-business/5-laws-of-advertising-and-marketing-that-haven-changed-in/289609); [SciMark, "In a TikTok world is longer still…"](https://scimark.substack.com/p/in-a-tiktok-world-is-longer-still?open=false)
- Ogilvy on where marketing went wrong (his critique of "creativity" untethered from selling) is summarised in secondary newsletters. — [Growing Viral / Marketing Archive](https://growingviral.beehiiv.com/p/david-ogilvy-marketing-went-wrong)
- Modern confirmation that the headline carries disproportionate weight: in the Upworthy randomized archive, swapping only the headline for the same content produced reliable CTR differences. — [Robertson et al. 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10202797/)
- A marketing blog says a ConversionXL study of how many people read an article versus its title "lined up pretty well" with Ogilvy. I could not locate the primary CXL data. Treat as weak. — [ReferralCandy](https://referralcandy.com/blog/david-ogilvy-ads)

### Inferences
- For a feed ad, Ogilvy's "headline" is now three things: the first 1 to 3 seconds of video, the on-image text overlay, and the first line of primary text before "…see more". The rule transfers. Arguably it is stronger now, since most impressions never expand the copy.
- "The consumer isn't a moron" gives the system a guardrail: no empty superlatives or slogan-only ads. Give a reason and a fact. This matches Hopkins.
- Long copy: in a feed, the image or hook stops the scroll and the long text serves only those who are already interested. Long copy costs little when collapsed, so "long body, short hook" is compatible with both Ogilvy and mobile constraints. Treat copy length as a testable variable, not a rule.
- "Brand image" and Ogilvy's TV rules (e.g. show the product, mention the brand early) are mostly relevant to larger advertisers. For small service businesses the transferable version is consistency: the same face, colours, name and promise in every ad.

### Gaps
- Exact page references for the 5x headline line, the "moron" line and the long-copy line were not verified against scans.
- Ogilvy's specific TV and print rules (e.g. "show the product", the "Big Idea", captions under photos being read more than body copy, his Starch-based readership claims) were not retrieved from primary text in this session.
- The basis of Ogilvy's 5:1 ratio (presumably Starch readership data) was not confirmed. No modern replication of the ratio in digital ads was found.
- I found no rigorous modern meta-analysis of copy length in paid social. Platform guidance and practitioner tests are mixed, and none were verified here.

## 3. John Caples (Tested Advertising Methods, 1932, later editions revised): headline structures, the role of testing, and "They laughed when I sat down at the piano"

### Takeaway
Caples is the patron saint of headline split-testing. His piano ad, written for the U.S. School of Music mail-order course in the mid-1920s, is a story-plus-transformation template ("they doubted me, then I proved them wrong") that has been reused for a century. Its direct-response pedigree is documented. The specific test numbers often quoted for it are not verified.

### Cited Findings
- The ad was for the U.S. School of Music, a mail-order music-lesson course. WARC's history describes it as a classic mail-order ad, and Frank Rowsome Jr. called it "the most beloved of all mail-order ads" and took the title of his 1959 advertising history from it. — [WARC case](https://www.warc.com/content/article/gale-emmc/us-school-of-music-they-laughed-when-i-sat-down-at-the-piano-campaign/en-gb/84554)
- Date conflict: Bridgeman Images dates a Caples print of the ad to 1925, while many secondary sources say 1926. — [Bridgeman Images](https://www.bridgemanimages.com/en-US/caples/they-laughed-when-i-sat-down-at-the-piano-but-when-i-started-to-play-advert-for-self-taught-music/lithograph/asset/390772)
- The storyline ran for several years. Variants of the piano and laughter U.S. School of Music ads appear in fan magazines from 1927 to 1928, which is consistent with a winning, keyed ad being rerun. — [Screenland 1927, Media History Digital Library](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/screenland15unse_0181); [Picture-Play 1928](https://lantern.mediahist.org/catalog/pictureplaymagaz29unse_0387)

### Inferences
- Caples's transferable patterns for an AI system are: (a) the underdog transformation story ("They laughed when… but when I…"); (b) news and "how to" headlines; (c) self-interest headlines that name the reader and the benefit; (d) above all, testing headlines against each other. Patterns (a) to (c) are recalled from Caples's book and are not verified here (see Gaps).
- The piano ad works as a template for coaches and consultants: a before-state of social embarrassment or doubt, a turning point, a credible mechanism (the course), then a call to action. It carries over well to UGC-style video testimonials.

### Gaps
- I could not verify Caples's exact wording on headline categories, his statement that one ad can sell 19.5 times as much as another (a widely repeated figure), or which edition introduced which chapter. *Tested Advertising Methods* was first published in 1932 and revised (4th and 5th editions, the 5th revised by Fred Hahn in 1997). These dates are recalled, not verified in this session.
- Whether the piano ad itself was split-tested against alternative headlines is not documented in the sources found.

## 4. Eugene Schwartz (Breakthrough Advertising, 1966): five levels of awareness, market sophistication stages, channelling existing desire. How is this used today?

### Takeaway
Schwartz's frameworks are widely taught in modern direct-response and funnel marketing: awareness levels for picking the headline's starting point, sophistication for picking claim vs mechanism vs identification. They are practitioner heuristics, and I found no academic validation. They are most useful to an AI system as a classifier that picks the hook type for each audience temperature (cold, warm, retargeting).

### Cited Findings
- Secondary sources consistently list five awareness stages: Unaware, Problem Aware, Solution Aware, Product Aware, Most Aware. The Most Aware prospect knows the product and needs only the offer or a small push. — [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/?p=80159); [Optimize Smart](https://optimizesmart.com/blog/schwartz-five-stages-of-awareness-in-marketing/); [Selzee guide](https://selzee.com/eugene-schwartz-5-levels-of-awareness)
- Applied today to lead generation and funnel mapping: cold traffic gets problem-led hooks, retargeting gets offer-led hooks. — [LeadGen Economy](https://www.leadgen-economy.com/blog/five-stages-awareness-lead-generation/); [Adbeat blog](https://blog.adbeat.com/?p=804)
- Market sophistication: secondary sources describe it as tailoring the claim to how familiar the market already is with similar claims. — [Breakthrough Advertising guide](https://siegfriedt-survey.umb.edu/pti/breakthrough-advertising-eugene-schwartz-pdf-a-deep-dive-into-the-hidden-details)
- One source dates the book to 1952, which is wrong. 1966 is the standard date. — [same guide, conflicting](https://siegfriedt-survey.umb.edu/pti/breakthrough-advertising-eugene-schwartz-pdf-a-deep-dive-into-the-hidden-details)

### Inferences
- Mapping for a small-service AI ad system:
  - Unaware or problem aware (cold social): lead with the pain or symptom, or a surprising fact. Do not lead with the brand name or price.
  - Solution aware: lead with the mechanism (why this approach works when others failed).
  - Product or most aware (retargeting, past leads): lead with the offer, the deadline and proof.
- Sophistication for a local service (plumber, dentist, roofer) is usually low to moderate, so direct claims and specifics win. Coaching and agency markets are highly sophisticated, so a unique mechanism and identification with the reader, rather than bigger claims, are needed. This is an inference from Schwartz's framework, not a tested result.
- Note that "channel existing desire, don't create it" agrees with Ehrenberg-Bass's view that advertising mostly works by reaching category buyers when they are ready (see Section 8).

### Gaps
- Exact Schwartz wording could not be verified. The commonly quoted lines are "Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already-existing desires onto a particular product", and the five-stage sophistication ladder (claim, enlarged claim, mechanism, enlarged mechanism, identification).
- No academic tests of awareness-level matching were found. Platform retargeting data supports the general idea of matching message to funnel stage, but nothing was retrieved here.

## 5. Bill Bernbach / DDB: creativity as the multiplier of effectiveness, Volkswagen "Think Small" and Avis "We try harder", honesty, and the art director + copywriter team

### Takeaway
Bernbach's 1947 thesis that persuasion is an art, and that executional originality is what makes a message noticed, is the best-supported "creative" claim in the canon. IPA databank analysis (Field, 2010s) found that creatively awarded campaigns were about 11 to 12 times more efficient at driving market share than non-awarded ones. Most of that evidence comes from large brands and long-term effects.

### Cited Findings
- Bernbach's letter to Grey Advertising is dated 15 May 1947. Its central line, in substance: advertising is fundamentally persuasion, and persuasion "happens to be not a science, but an art". Transcriptions differ in minor wording. — [Letters of Note](https://news.lettersofnote.com/p/let-us-blaze-new-trails); [DMA](https://dma.org.uk/article/bernbach-s-letter-to-grey); [Branding Strategy Insider](https://www.brandingstrategyinsider.com/advertisings-le/)
- The Volkswagen campaign was created by DDB art director Helmut Krone and copywriter Julian Koenig, an example of the art director + copywriter pairing Bernbach institutionalized. One source says Ad Age named it the best campaign of the century. I could not confirm this independently. — [Marketing.ie, "Bernbach to the future"](https://marketing.ie/articles/bernbach-to-the-future/)
- Peter Field's analysis of the IPA Effectiveness Databank found that creatively awarded campaigns (those in the Gunn Report) grew market share about 11 times more efficiently than non-awarded ones. — [WARC, Field](https://www.warc.com/newsandopinion/news/strong-creative-campaigns-deliver-best-results/en-gb/26851)
- The efficiency gap was about 3x for 1994 to 2002 and about 12x for 2003 to 2010. — [Smart Insights](https://www.smartinsights.com/online-brand-strategy/brand-development/the-secret-campaign-ingredient/)
- Awarded campaigns were much more likely to be emotional than rational (44% vs 19%). Field argued that emotional bias explains only a small part of their superiority. — [Marketing Society, "The value of creativity"](https://marketingsociety.com/the-library/value-creativity)
- Awarded campaigns achieved this with less "excess share of voice" than non-awarded ones, so the advantage is not explained by bigger budgets. — [WARC](https://www.warc.com/newsandopinion/news/strong-creative-campaigns-deliver-best-results/en-gb/26851)
- Sample size conflict: summaries cite 435 campaigns (1994 to 2010) or 175 campaigns (2000 to 2008). — [Marketing Society](https://marketingsociety.com/the-library/value-creativity); [Smart Insights](https://www.smartinsights.com/online-brand-strategy/brand-development/the-secret-campaign-ingredient/)
- Nielsen Catalina Solutions (2017), across about 450 to 500 CPG campaigns: creative accounted for about 47% of sales lift from advertising, plus 2% from context, versus 36% for media (reach 22%, targeting 9%, recency 5%) and 15% for brand. The earlier Apollo Project (2006) put creative at about 65%. On TV, media mattered more (50% vs 37%). — [MarketingCharts](https://www.marketingcharts.com/television-80662)

### Inferences
- For a rulebook: creative quality is the largest lever the advertiser controls, roughly half of measured sales lift. An AI system should spend its effort on generating and testing distinct concepts rather than micro-optimising targeting.
- Bernbach's honesty principle (VW's self-deprecation, Avis admitting it is No. 2) foreshadows the "damaging admission" technique in direct response, and it suits small service businesses: "We're a 3-person firm, so you talk to the owner."
- The art director + copywriter pairing translates for an AI system into always generating visual and words as one concept, not writing copy and then picking stock imagery.
- Caveat: "awarded" ≠ "weird". Field's data concern big brands with media budgets, and the gains appear mostly in long-term share. Do not use these figures to justify unclear, clever ads for a local plumber.

### Gaps
- Primary sources for "Think Small" (1959) and Avis "We try harder" (1962) copy and dates were not retrieved. The dates are well known but not verified here.
- Commonly attributed Bernbach lines ("Rules are what the artist breaks…"; "The truth isn't the truth until people believe you…") were not verified, and some are likely embellished or apocryphal.

## 6. Wieden+Kennedy and "Just Do It"; brand voice and cultural relevance

### Takeaway
"Just Do It" (1988) is the archetype of a brand line that expresses an attitude rather than a product claim. Its origin is documented in Dan Wieden's own account. It is a large-budget, decades-long brand play with almost no direct transfer to a small service business, except the lesson that a consistent voice and a stance compound over time.

### Cited Findings
- Dan Wieden said he adapted the line from Gary Gilmore's last words before his 1977 execution, "Let's do it" ("I didn't like 'Let's do it' so I just changed it to 'Just do it'"). He had read about them in Mailer's *The Executioner's Song*. — [The New Daily, 2015](https://www.thenewdaily.com.au/entertainment/2015/03/23/nikes-just-inspired-convicted-killer); [The Mercury](https://themercury.co.za/world/2015-03-19-how-killer-inspired-nike-slogan/)
- The line debuted in 1988 in a spot featuring 80-year-old runner Walt Stack. — [MediaVillage](https://mediavillage.com/article/historys-moment-in-media-the-rise-of-nikes-just-do-it-tagline/amp); [Boston Globe obituary of Wieden, 2022](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2022/10/07/nation/dan-wieden-adman-who-coined-just-do-it-nike-dies-77)
- Phil Knight was initially sceptical. Former Nike marketing chief Liz Dolan framed the line as "the ultimate statement of intention". — [The New Daily](https://www.thenewdaily.com.au/entertainment/2015/03/23/nikes-just-inspired-convicted-killer)

### Inferences
- The transferable lesson for small businesses is a consistent voice plus one repeated promise line. Ehrenberg-Bass-style "distinctive brand assets" (the same founder face, colour and phrase) give the small-budget version of brand building.
- Cultural relevance (topical hooks, trends) works on paid social as a scroll-stopper. It is risky for small advertisers because of tone mismatch, so treat it as an optional test variant, not a default.

### Gaps
- Nike sales growth attributed to the campaign (commonly cited as roughly $877M in 1988 to $9.2B in 1998) was not verified, and causal attribution to the slogan is untestable.
- No sources were retrieved on W+K's stated philosophy of brand voice.

## 7. Sugarman (slippery slide, psychological triggers), Halbert (starving crowd, A-pile), Kern (results in advance, persuasion-first, intent-based branding): what is distinctive and how does it apply to paid social?

### Takeaway
These three are direct-response practitioners from mail order, catalogues and the early internet. Sugarman contributed the idea that every element exists only to get the next one read. Halbert contributed "market first" (a starving crowd beats a clever ad) and personal-letter formats that get opened. Kern contributed value-first video ads aimed at engaged audiences, with retargeting. All three are unvalidated academically. Kern's model maps most directly onto modern paid social.

### Cited Findings
- Sugarman: *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook* (Wiley, 2006) has a chapter titled "The Slippery Slide". The principle as summarised: "the sole purpose of the first sentence in an advertisement is to get you to read the second sentence". Summary wording, not verified text. Copyblogger quotes Sugarman's seminar teaching that a reader should go "down a slippery slide" leading to the offer and the sale. — [Samuel Thomas Davies book notes](https://samuelthomasdavies.com/book-summaries/business/the-adweek-copywriting-handbook); [Wiley table of contents](https://gov.wiley.com/WileyCDA/WileyTitle/productCd-0470051248,descCd-tableOfContents.html); [Copyblogger Copywriting 101 PDF](https://copyblogger.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Copywriting101-4.pdf); [Alexandra Allen Substack](https://alexandraallen.substack.com/p/the-slide-and-the-guinea-pig)
- Halbert: *The Boron Letters* were letters to his son Bond written from Boron Federal Prison Camp starting in 1982. The "A-pile" principle (mail that looks personal gets opened, while obvious advertising goes in the B-pile and is thrown away) comes from this tradition. — [BeFreed summary](https://www.befreed.ai/fr/book/the-boron-letters-by-gary-c-halbert)
- Kern's Intent-Based Branding, in secondary summaries: run short, helpful educational videos as paid social ads, then retarget engaged viewers, rather than forcing cold traffic through an opt-in gate. — [copy.ai on intent-based marketing](https://www.copy.ai/blog/intent-based-marketing); [BMA newsletter on Kern and branding](https://bma.beehiiv.com/p/more-on-branding-and-frank-kern); [Kern IBB training video](https://loom.com/embed/0c9f518d68684a5ca276740e4bca24a4)
- A Kern podcast summary splits the market as roughly 5% ready to buy now, 50% ready within about 90 days and 45% six months or more out. IBB targets the middle group. These are Kern's heuristic figures, not data. — [Your Next Million podcast, "Zero Resistance Marketing"](https://podcastaddict.com/your-next-million/episode/221610759)
- An independent analogue from marketing science: Ehrenberg-Bass's John Dawes (2021) argued that about 95% of B2B buyers are out-of-market at any time, because suppliers are switched only about every five years. — [Blck Alpaca summary of the 95-5 rule](https://blckalpaca.at/en/knowledge-base/social-media/social-media-fundamentals-strategy/brand-vs-performance-marketing-95-5-rule)
- The internal Build Room skills in this repo already implement Halbert (starving crowd, greased slide, A-pile, 40/40/20) and Kern's eight-step persuasion-first framework, assigning Kern to short pieces and Halbert to long-form. — local file `/home/user/codex/buildroom/skills/buildroom-copy-engine/SKILL.md` (internal, not an external source)

### Inferences
- What is distinctive about each, and how it applies to paid social:
  - Sugarman: the first line exists to earn the second. In a feed this becomes hook → retain → click, which matches how platforms reward watch time and thumb-stop rate. It is the strongest transfer of the three.
  - Halbert: market before message. For ad targeting this means picking an audience that already has an urgent problem (e.g. "homeowners with a roof leak", "coaches whose launch flopped") before writing a word. The A-pile becomes native-looking creative: ads that look like a post from a person (selfie video, plain text) rather than a polished ad. Treat this as a hypothesis to test, though many practitioners report it.
  - Kern: give value first and retarget the engaged. This is a natural fit for small service businesses with long consideration cycles (consultants, agencies) and remarkably close to Ehrenberg-Bass's 95-5 logic. It reached a similar conclusion from a different evidence base.
- "Results in advance" (Kern) is generally understood as giving prospects a real, small win before they buy, so the purchase feels like a continuation. I could not find a primary definition (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- Primary sources for Halbert's "starving crowd" story (the hamburger stand question, from his newsletter or seminars) could not be found. Searches returned nothing that verifies it; it is widely repeated but secondhand.
- Kern's phrase "results in advance" could not be traced to a primary Kern source in this session.
- Sugarman's list of psychological triggers (from *Triggers*, 1999, and the Adweek handbook) was not retrieved.
- No independent tests of any of these three frameworks were found.

## 8. Where do these traditions conflict, and how should a system resolve conflicts for small service businesses?

### Takeaway
The main conflicts are brand vs direct response, emotional vs rational, long vs short copy, and clever vs clear. Marketing science (IPA, Binet and Field, Ehrenberg-Bass) says long-term growth needs emotional, broad-reach brand building alongside short-term activation. Typically about 60:40 for B2C and roughly 46:54 for B2B. But those numbers come from large advertisers. Small service businesses with small budgets and immediate cash needs should be activation-heavy, with a brand layer of consistent distinctive assets and value-first content.

### Cited Findings
- Binet and Field, from IPA effectiveness cases: an optimal split of about 60% brand building to 40% activation (later 62:38). The B2B refinement, with the LinkedIn B2B Institute (2019), is about 46:54. Byron Sharp has criticised the 60/40 rule as unscientific and based on biased award data. — [Blck Alpaca explainer](https://blckalpaca.at/en/knowledge-base/social-media/social-media-fundamentals-strategy/brand-vs-performance-marketing-95-5-rule); [MarketingEdge on Ritson / Binet and Field B2B formula](https://marketingedge.com.ng/the-b2b-marketing-growth-formula-from-mark-ritson-les-binet-and-peter-field/); [WARC, Binet and Field B2B lessons](https://www.warc.com/content/article/event-reports/binet-and-field-effectiveness-lessons-in-b2b/130287)
- Emotional campaigns, particularly highly creative ones, produce stronger long-term business effects than rational campaigns (Binet and Field, *The Long and Short of It*, as summarised by Ipsos). — [Ipsos ASI paper](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/publication/1970-01/asi-nurturinggreatcreative-sept2013.pdf)
- Emotional campaigns are overrepresented among creatively awarded, high-efficiency campaigns (44% vs 19%). — [Marketing Society](https://marketingsociety.com/the-library/value-creativity)
- Out-of-market logic (95-5): most buyers are not in-market at any moment, which supports memory-building ads alongside lead capture. — [Blck Alpaca](https://blckalpaca.at/en/knowledge-base/social-media/social-media-fundamentals-strategy/brand-vs-performance-marketing-95-5-rule)
- On the direct-response side, Ogilvy himself cited direct-response advertisers knowing "to a dollar" what each ad sells as the evidence for long copy. — [SciMark](https://scimark.substack.com/p/in-a-tiktok-world-is-longer-still?open=false)

### Inferences
Proposed conflict-resolution rules for a small-service AI ad system (inferences, not tested results):
1. **Brand vs DR:** default to direct response (an offer, a CTA, a measurable result) because cash flow and attribution matter at small scale. Embed brand inside every DR ad: the same founder face, colours, name and tagline (distinctive assets). Reserve 20 to 40% of creative variants for value-first, no-ask content (Kern / IBB) that builds the retargeting pool and memory for the 95% out-of-market.
2. **Emotional vs rational:** use an emotional hook (the problem felt, the identity, the before/after story à la Caples) to open, and a rational body (Hopkins's specifics, proof, mechanism, price/risk reversal). This sequencing honours both traditions.
3. **Long vs short:** let awareness level decide (Schwartz). Cold, unaware audiences get a short hook and a low-friction offer. Warm and high-consideration audiences (for high-ticket coaching) can take long copy or long video. Always test length rather than legislate it.
4. **Clever vs clear:** clarity first. A clever line must still state the benefit within the first line or overlay. The creativity multiplier (Field) applies to distinctiveness and emotional power, not obscurity.
5. **Testing as tie-breaker:** whenever traditions conflict, the system generates both variants and lets keyed results decide. This is Hopkins and Caples applied to their own disputes.

### Gaps
- No published effectiveness data specific to micro/small service businesses was found. The IPA and NCS evidence is drawn from large and CPG advertisers, so the transfer is inferred.
- The original Binet and Field reports (*The Long and Short of It*, 2013; *Media in Focus*; *Effectiveness in Context*) were not read directly.

## 9. Which classic rules are weakened or changed by mobile-first, sound-off, scroll-feed environments?

### Takeaway
Rules about the reader's attention (headline primacy, the first sentence earns the second, specificity, the visual + verbal concept) are strengthened in the feed. Rules that assume a captive, paging reader or a TV viewer listening (long body copy as the main vehicle, the sound-carried jingle or voiceover, slow brand reveals) are weakened. Sound-off viewing means the message must work visually and in captions.

### Cited Findings
- In 2016, publishers reported that up to 85% of Facebook video views were with sound off (LittleThings and Mic about 85%, PopSugar 50 to 80%, and MEC's branded videos 85 to 90% silent). This came from publishers, not official Meta data, and it dates from 2016. — [Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/?web=1); [Marketing Dive](https://marketingdive.com/news/most-of-facebooks-8b-daily-video-views-happen-without-sound/419407)
- MEC's internal studies found brand lift and purchase intent were not affected by sound on vs off when creative was built for the feed. Digiday warned that repurposed TV-style ads may underperform. — [Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/?web=1)
- Media's share of sales-lift contribution has grown (NCS 2017 vs Apollo 2006), but creative remained the single largest factor at about 47 to 49%. — [MarketingCharts](https://www.marketingcharts.com/television-80662)

### Inferences
Rules for an AI ad system:
- **Strengthened:**
  - Ogilvy's headline primacy, now the first 1 to 3 seconds plus the text overlay.
  - Sugarman's slippery slide, applied to retention curves.
  - Hopkins's specificity: numbers read fast on a small screen.
  - Caples's testing: platforms make split tests cheap.
- **Changed:**
  - Ogilvy's "brand early" rule for TV has become "brand or face early, plus a burned-in caption".
  - Ogilvy's photo-caption observation has become "on-image text carries the message".
- **Weakened:**
  - Long copy as the main persuader, which now moves to the landing page or the expanded text.
  - Audio-dependent devices (jingles, voiceover-only claims). Every video needs captions and a frame-1 visual hook.
  - Elaborate print layouts.
- **Unchanged:** the "consumer isn't a moron" and honesty principles. Platform ad policies and audience scepticism arguably make them more important.
- Flag for the rulebook: Hopkins, Caples, Schwartz, Halbert and Sugarman built their rules in mail-order print and direct mail, where the reader had already chosen to read the page. In a feed the ad interrupts. The opening has to earn attention before any of their body-copy rules apply.

### Gaps
- No current (2024 to 2026) official Meta, TikTok or YouTube statistic on sound-off share was retrieved. The 85% figure is a decade old and from publishers.
- No primary-source data on captions' effect on conversions was verified. One aggregator cites 12% and 40% caption lifts with no primary links, so those are excluded.
- No controlled study of feed-ad copy length (short vs long primary text) was retrieved.
