# Speaking Engine: outside research, November 2026

Research date: 2026-11-14. Audience for the skill: non-technical service business owners aged 50+ (coaches, consultants, bookkeepers, trades, local professionals), mostly US.

## Read this first: how far to trust these notes

**Method limit.** This session could run web searches but could not open pages. Every direct page fetch failed at the network level (pcma.org, allamericanspeakers.com, td.org and ftc.gov were all unreachable from the session). So every finding below comes from a search-result summary and quoted snippet of the linked page, not from reading the page in full. Numbers are reported as the search surfaced them. Before any number goes into the skill as a stated fact, someone should open the link and confirm it. Where two sources disagreed, both are shown.

**Tags.**

- **[data]** a study, survey or count with a named source. Also used, marked "(policy)", for the text of a named rule, law or published policy, since those are primary documents and not opinion.
- **[practitioner]** a named practitioner's or vendor's stated practice or claim. Not evidence that it works.
- **[folklore]** widely repeated; no source found, or the source does not say what is claimed.
- **UNCONFIRMED** looked for and not found, or found only second-hand.

**Bias to keep in mind.** Almost every "industry survey" in this field is published by a party that sells to speakers or planners (bureaus, speaker CRMs, podcast booking agencies, training companies). The surveys sample their own lists, so they describe established paid keynote speakers, not a first-time local speaker. No source found describes the Build Room member's tier with data.

## 1. Where speakers get booked, by tier

### 1a. Free local stages

**Rotary clubs**

- [data] (policy) Decision-maker is the club's program chair or program committee; one Rotary program-chair writer says the chair "is usually given final authority to accept or reject a speaker". https://buildingtherotarybrand.blogspot.com/2015/12/part-3-insider-tips-for-building-strong.html
- [data] (policy) Format is a talk of about 20 minutes plus questions. Glendora: programs "typically 20-25 minutes"; Hamilton: "maximum of 20 minutes"; Arcadia: 20 minutes with Q&A; one club reports a typical 25 to 30 attendees. https://www.rotaryofglendora.org/guest-speakers.php , https://rotaryclubhamilton.ca/guest-speaker-form/ , https://www.arcadiarotary.org/guest-speaker-application/
- [data] (policy) Many clubs ban selling. Glendora: "commercial or promotional sales presentations are not accepted". Ann Arbor: no "member, speaker, guest or other person may sell products or tickets or solicit donations at club meetings" except for the club and Rotary causes. Other clubs' guidelines bar "making a sales pitch or directly soliciting purchases or donations". https://www.a2rotary.org/?p=14956 , https://dacdb.com/Rotary/Accounts/5130/Downloads/402/Speaker%20Guidelines%20v.3.0.pdf
- These are individual club policies. UNCONFIRMED: a single Rotary International rule on speaker solicitation. Rules vary by club; the member must ask each club.
- Pay: none found. Rotary speaking is unpaid. UNCONFIRMED how far ahead clubs book (no sourced figure; clubs meet weekly so calendars fill a few weeks to a few months ahead, which is practitioner lore).

**Chambers of commerce**

- [data] (policy) Chambers that publish speaker forms consistently require educational, non-promotional content. Mint Hill: presenters must avoid "'sales pitches' about a particular product or service". Wichita: "this is not a promotional pitch for the presenters". Tri-Lakes asks for a title, description and 2 to 3 key takeaways and says submission "does not constitute an agreement" to schedule. https://business.minthillchamberofcommerce.com/form/view/27581 , https://www.wichitachamber.org/forms/speaker-proposal-form/ , https://trilakeschamber.com/host---speaker-proposals
- [data] (policy) Often members only, and rationed. Mint Hill limits members to "one speaking opportunity per 24 month period"; Huntsville required "Chamber Members in good standing". https://cm.hsvchamber.org/events/details/2015-speaker-portal-03-02-2015-2288
- [data] (policy) Unpaid. Fargo-Moorhead: speakers "will not receive monetary compensation". https://www.fmchamber.com/call-for-presenters
- Implication: for chambers the real cost of the stage is the membership fee. The skill should say so.

**BNI**

- [data] (policy) BNI chapters keep "a featured speaker list scheduled at least six weeks in advance", run by the chapter's leadership team. https://www.bni.com/?p=17949
- UNCONFIRMED whether a non-member can give a featured presentation. BNI's featured presentation is a member slot whose stated purpose is to generate referrals; visitors are guests, not booked speakers. Treat BNI as a membership-based referral group, not as a stage to pitch.

**Public libraries**

- [data] (policy) Libraries take program proposals through a form. Lead times stated by individual libraries: "at least three months in advance", "at least eight weeks in advance", and one schedules "as far as nine months ahead". https://greentreelibrary.org/events/program-proposals.html , https://www.cityofmelissa.com/DocumentCenter/View/1809/Programming-Request-Form
- [data] (policy) Pay varies and is small. Greenville County Library System "may pay an invited program presenter an honorarium of at least $75.00"; New Orleans and Boston ask on the form whether an honorarium is requested; others are unpaid. https://www.greenvillelibrary.org/policies/29/public-program-presenter-policy , https://nolalibrary.libanswers.com/events/faq/431683 , https://denverlibrary.org/present
- Libraries are public bodies: expect a no-selling rule and no list capture on site. UNCONFIRMED as a universal rule; check each library's policy.

**Toastmasters**

- [data] (policy) Toastmasters is practice, not booking. Its Accredited Speaker credential requires a minimum of 25 speaking engagements to non-Toastmasters audiences (one form says 15 of the 25 must be paid, audiences of 20 or more) and five client recommendations. Versions of the rule differ; the handbook is the authority. https://www.toastmasters.org/resources/accredited-speaker-program-handbook , https://toastmasters.org/membership/accredited-speaker/become-an-accredited-speaker
- UNCONFIRMED: district-level Toastmasters "speakers bureaus" that place free speakers with community groups. Some districts run them; no central source found.

**Meetups and other local groups.** UNCONFIRMED. No sourced data found on who decides or how far ahead. Organisers decide; treat anything else as lore.

### 1b. Associations and trade conferences (call for speakers / call for proposals)

- [data] (policy) Timing, from 2027 calls visible now: AAAL (event March 2027) opened 1 June 2026, closed 15 July 2026, about 8 months out. ALA (June 2027) announced its call 24 August 2026, about 10 months out. SIOP (2027) submissions 2 September to 7 October 2026. MLA (April 2027) call ran 24 August to 9 October 2026. ACRP 2027 call closed 8 June 2026 with decisions in August. SHRM-Atlanta (31 March to 1 April 2027) closes 15 November 2026, about 4.5 months out. Pattern: calls open roughly 9 to 10 months before the event and close roughly 6 to 8 months before; state and chapter events run shorter, 4 to 6 months. https://www.aaal.org/2027-call-for-proposals , https://www.ala.org/news/2026/08/ala-opens-2027-annual-conference-exhibition-call-proposals , https://www.siop.org/post/2027-siop-annual-conference-call-for-proposals-now-available/ , https://sessionize.com/2027-mla-annual-conference , https://acrpnet.org/call-for-proposals , https://shrmsav.wildapricot.org/2026-Call-For-Speakers
- Consequence for the skill: a member who starts in November is generally pitching association stages for the second half of next year or the year after. Most 2027 spring calls are already closed.
- [data] (policy) Who decides: a volunteer review committee scoring against a rubric. AIEA: "reviewed by multiple members of the conference committee". ASSP scores "the degree to which the proposed session meets the conference/program objectives, desirability of the topic, and the speaker's presentation skills and experience". https://www.aieaworld.org/aieacfpassessmentrubric.html , https://www.assp.org/education/conferences/leadership-conference/call-for-presenters
- [data] (policy) Pay: breakout speakers are usually unpaid. Wisconsin SHRM: "We DO NOT offer a speaker honorarium nor cover transportation costs". South Carolina SHRM: presenters "WILL NOT RECEIVE AN HONORARIUM" unless a keynote exception is made. What they give instead is complimentary registration (ASSP, Independent Sector for members). https://www.wishrm.org/2026-speaking-opportunities , https://summit.independentsector.org/frequently-asked-questions
- [data] An older but direct survey of associations: Tagoras and Velvet Chainsaw, "The Speaker Report" (December 2011), found nearly 84 percent of responding associations hire professional speakers (typically keynotes), and has sections on calls for proposals and on compensating industry speakers. It is 15 years old; the detailed pay figures were not retrievable. https://es.slideshare.net/ideasandrew/tagoras-velvetchainsawspeakerreport
- [data] Innovation Women reports that half of the event managers it surveyed "had absolutely no budget for speakers". Sample and date not retrieved. https://innovationwomen.com/dear-innovation-women-tell-me-more-about-how-and-if-speakers-get-paid/

### 1c. Corporate

- [data] AAE Speakers Bureau, 2024 Speaking Industry Benchmark Report (340 organisers, 378 speakers): choosing a speaker is collaborative, "less than 8 percent of respondents make the booking decisions alone". Organisers start looking "at least 4 months in advance"; the 2023 edition said nearly half start 6+ months ahead for in-person events. https://www.pcma.org/survey-explores-speaker-planner-dynamics/ , https://www.allamericanspeakers.com/blog/speaking-industry-report-2024-six-key-takeaways/ , https://www.bizbash.com/event-production-planning/planning-your-2023-programming-here-s-8-tips-for-booking-event-speakers-now
- [data] Same report: average overall budget for a keynote speaker $22,449; most common range (47 percent) $10,000 to $50,000. This is the bureau-booked keynote market, not the member's market.
- UNCONFIRMED: who inside a company books a small outside expert for a lunch-and-learn (HR, L&D, a department head, an employee resource group). No survey found. Practitioner lore only.
- UNCONFIRMED: a 2025 or 2026 edition of the AAE report. Searches found only 2022, 2023 and 2024.

### 1d. Speakers bureaus

- [practitioner] Bureaus are paid by commission on bookings and do not create demand for unknown speakers. The Speaker Lab: "if you feel like you need a bureau to get gigs, they probably aren't interested in you". One bureau states it wants "a minimum gross booking revenue of $300K" before exclusive representation. https://thespeakerlab.com/blog/5-things-you-should-know-about-speakers-bureaus/ , https://www.bigspeak.com/about/keynote-speakers-faqs-for-speakers-bureaus/
- [practitioner] Bureaus ask for video above all: "the most important thing is to have a good video". https://www.executivespeakers.com/blogdetails/tuesday-tip-how-to-get-noticed-by-a-speakers-bureau
- For this member: bureaus are out of scope for the first year or more. The skill should say so plainly and move on.

### 1e. Podcasts (as a guest)

- [data] Reach: Edison Research, Infinite Dial 2025 (5,020 people aged 12+): 55 percent of Americans 12+ listen to a podcast monthly, about 158 million. https://www.edisonresearch.com/the-infinite-dial-2025/ , https://podnews.net/press-release/edison-research-infinite-dial-2025
- [data] Let's Make A Podcast (a guest-matching marketplace, so interested), study published 12 July 2026, parsed 314 podcast feeds: only 32 percent publish anything showing they book outside guests; 23 percent had not published in roughly six months; larger independent shows scored as more approachable than small ones. https://soundsprofitable.com/?p=12716
- Who decides: the host, or a producer on larger shows. Pay: normally none either way (see section 6).
- UNCONFIRMED: lead time from booking to air. One blog attributes "8.4 weeks from recording to release" to Sounds Profitable; not verified.

### 1f. Virtual summits and guest webinars

- [practitioner] Summit speakers are generally unpaid and are expected to promote. One summit's terms require "a solo email and a social post" and offer a 30 percent affiliate commission on paid upgrade sales. Line-ups run from 5 to 50 speakers. https://heysummit.com/blog/event-affiliate-program , https://summithosthangout.libsyn.com/104-do-you-have-to-pay-virtual-summit-speakers-affiliate-commissions
- UNCONFIRMED: any stated minimum email-list size that hosts require. Widely said; no source found. [folklore] until sourced.
- Consequence: a summit is a list-swap, not a stage. A member with no list has little to trade, and one with a list is being asked to lend it. If the member promotes for a commission, FTC disclosure rules apply (section 8).

## 2. Fees

**Caution.** Most fee tables online ("beginner $500 to $2,500, intermediate $5,000 to $10,000...") give no source, sample or date. They are [folklore]. Sites such as talks.co and worldmetrics.org publish fee aggregates with no method and should not be used. Nothing below describes what a first-time local speaker is paid, because no survey samples that group.

- [data] AAE 2023 report: average speaker fee $15,551 for in-person events; $16,659 for speakers with 10+ years' experience. 2024 report via PCMA: average fee reported by speaker respondents "around $14,000". Sample is experienced speakers on a bureau's list. https://www.meetingsnet.com/speakers-entertainment/hot-topics-top-fees-2023-speaker-study , https://www.pcma.org/survey-explores-speaker-planner-dynamics/
- [data] SpeakerFlow, State of the Speaking Industry 2021: 69.6 percent of respondents made less than $5,000 per engagement in 2020 (55.2 percent in 2019); the average speaker earned $50,000 to $75,000 in 2020. Pandemic-year data, own-list sample. https://speakerflow.com/the-state-of-the-speaking-industry-2021-report
- [data] SpeakerFlow 2026 report: 216 respondents in 13 countries; the largest group, nearly a third, earns $100,000 to $250,000 a year; 65 percent say more revenue is their top priority. Per-engagement fee figures not retrievable. https://pages.speakerflow.com/2026-ssi-report/
- [practitioner] National Speakers Association article (Crystal Washington, CSP, CPAE, 2025) describes a "Business Builder" as booking 10 to 20 engagements a year at "a steady four-figure fee". Tiers, not survey data. https://nsaspeaker.org/lets-get-candid-about-keynote-speaking-fees/
- UNCONFIRMED: any NSA member income or fee survey. None found.
- UNCONFIRMED: any sourced fee for first-time or emerging speakers. The honest statement is "local and association breakout stages usually pay nothing; some libraries pay a small honorarium (one policy: at least $75); paid work at four figures comes after a track record, and the published averages describe established keynote speakers".

**What bureaus take**

- [practitioner] Commission is commonly quoted at 20 to 30 percent of the fee (some say 25 to 35, some 20 to 25), built into the quoted fee, with normally no upfront cost to the speaker. All sources are bureaus or vendors; no neutral survey. An upfront listing fee is a warning sign (section 7). https://speakerflow.com/how-does-a-speakers-bureau-work/ , https://www.bigspeak.com/about/faq/speakers-bureaus-faqs/

**What "free" stages give instead**

- [data] (policy) Complimentary registration is the standard association benefit (ASSP, Independent Sector). https://www.assp.org/education/conferences/leadership-conference/call-for-presenters
- [practitioner] Travel is often not covered. "For most conferences, the majority of speakers only get the conference fee waived"; at commercial web conferences speakers "more often than not" bear their own expenses. https://www.freecodecamp.org/news/how-conference-speakers-get-compensated-93b49fd370f1/ , https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2018/12/state-of-commercial-web-conferences-events/
- Recording, attendee list, back-of-room sales, a sponsored slot: all are negotiable asks, and all are frequently refused (attendee lists especially, for privacy reasons). UNCONFIRMED how often each is granted; no data found. The skill should present them as things to ask for in writing, never as things a host "gives".

## 3. What organisers look for, what makes them say no, one-sheets and reels

**What they look for**

- [data] AAE 2024: organisers' top priorities when booking are audience engagement (67 percent), education for the audience (49 percent) and staying within budget (about two in five). Attendance is their top measure of event success (three in four). https://www.pcma.org/survey-explores-speaker-planner-dynamics/
- [data] AAE 2022 (nearly 700 event professionals): whether the fee fits the budget was a top selection criterion at 75.5 percent. https://www.allamericanspeakers.com/blog/key-takeaways-from-aaes-2022-speaking-industry-benchmark-report/
- [data] Successful Meetings research: 87 percent of planners favour content relevant to the group, over motivational (55 percent) or entertaining (53 percent). Date not retrieved. https://www.successfulmeetings.com/News/Research-Whitepapers/Research-Talk-is-Serious-Business

**What makes them say no, or not rebook**

- [data] Speak Inc. and Jay Baer survey of 150 planners (2020): about three-quarters named boilerplate (uncustomised) speeches and bait-and-switch presentations as their top two annoyances. https://blog.speakinc.com/what-meeting-planners-want-and-9-things-that-drive-them-crazy
- [practitioner] Recurring irritants listed by planners: last-minute demands, running over time, self-promotion from the stage. https://www.expertclick.com/NewsRelease/What-Delights-and-What-Irks-Your-Meeting-Planner,2025311684.aspx
- [data] Meetings & Conventions survey of 242 professionals: two-thirds said unsolicited sales pitches from suppliers are "not very" or "not at all" useful. About suppliers generally, not speakers, and dated. https://www.meetings-conventions.com/News/Research/Planners-Peeved-by-Cold-Calls
- [data] AAE 2024, speaker side: nearly two-thirds of speakers said finding engagements is their biggest challenge, up from 52 percent in 2023. The market is crowded even for experienced speakers.
- UNCONFIRMED: any MPI, Cvent or Bizzabo survey on why planners reject speakers. None surfaced.

**One-sheet**

- [practitioner] Agreed contents: who you are, two or three talk titles each with a paragraph and the takeaways for attendees, the audiences you serve, past engagements and testimonials, a photo, how to book. Planner pages also carry an introduction script, AV needs and a pre-program questionnaire. https://thespeakerlab.com/blog/how-to-create-a-speaker-one-sheet/ , https://speakermatch.com/resources/speaker-guides/?i=16
- This matches what chambers actually ask for on their forms: title, description, 2 to 3 takeaways, expertise related to the topic (section 1a).

**Reel**

- [practitioner] Bureaus and paid keynote buyers want video: "planners and coordinators need to see you in action, especially when they find you without a referral". https://publicwords.com/?p=3213
- UNCONFIRMED and important: no survey found giving the share of planners who watch video before hiring, and no evidence found that a reel is required for Rotary, chamber, library or association breakout stages. The published application forms for those stages (section 1) ask for a topic, description, takeaways and bio; none retrieved asks for a reel. "You must have a sizzle reel before you pitch" is [folklore] for the local and association breakout tiers. A plain two-to-five minute clip of a real talk is the sensible first asset once one exists; that is a judgement, not a finding.

## 4. Pitching

**To a program chair or planner**

- [practitioner] The Speaker Lab: a pitch names the planner's audience, the problem that audience has, and the result the talk delivers. Wait two weeks, then follow up once by forwarding the original. https://thespeakerlab.com/blog/how-to-pitch-yourself/ , https://thespeakerlab.com/follow-up
- [practitioner] Other guides: follow up after 3 days, a week and 30 days; or once after 7 to 10 days; or 2 to 3 times a week apart. Practitioners disagree. There is no data on the right number for speaking pitches. https://speakerbureau.thelohm.org/node/84063
- UNCONFIRMED: ideal pitch length. Everyone says "short"; no study.
- Sourced guidance on content is the application forms themselves: lead with the audience's topic and takeaways, state expertise, say it is educational and not a sales presentation.

**Reply rates (sourced only)**

- No reply-rate data exists for speaker pitches specifically. The figures below are general B2B cold email from email-tool vendors and must not be presented as speaking benchmarks.
- [data] Hunter.io, State of Cold Email: average sequence reply rate 4.5 percent (4.1 percent in 2024). https://hunter.io/the-state-of-cold-email
- [data] Instantly: average reply rate 3.43 percent; top quartile 5.5 percent. https://instantly.ai/blog/cold-email-reply-rate-benchmarks
- [data] Belkins: 0.45 percent of 7,530,489 cold emails sent in 2025 got a reply (34,393). The same campaign can be "5 percent" measured against opens; denominators differ between vendors. https://belkins.io/blog/cold-email-response-rates.md
- [data] Vendors report a lift on the first follow-up (Instantly, citing Backlinko). Size of lift not retrieved.
- Honest framing for the member: expect most cold pitches to get no reply; low single-digit reply rates are normal for cold email in general; a warm introduction or membership changes this a great deal, though that last point is practitioner consensus without a number.

**Call-for-speakers submissions: what committees score**

- [data] (policy) ASSP: fit with conference objectives, desirability of the topic, the speaker's presentation skills and experience. https://www.assp.org/education/conferences/leadership-conference/call-for-presenters
- [data] (policy) AIEA rubric: proposals "must not focus on 'selling/advocating' a particular service/product/program" and must not be only "show and tell". https://www.aieaworld.org/aieacfpassessmentrubric.html
- [data] (policy) Independent Sector: sessions scored on a rubric, first item alignment with the event's aims. ALA publishes a scoring description per criterion. ATD publishes its own selection criteria (page not readable from this session). https://summit.independentsector.org/frequently-asked-questions , https://www.td.org/atd-international-conference-and-expo-proposal-selection-criteria
- [data] (policy) Chamber criteria: "session objectives are clearly articulated and achievable in the time allotted" and "expertise is related to the session content". https://scwcc.com/speaker-request-for-proposal/
- Reading across them: relevance to this audience and theme, clear learning objectives, evidence the speaker can deliver, and absence of sales content. Write to the rubric the call publishes.

## 5. Speaking to get clients

**What hosts forbid**

- [data] (policy) AcademyHealth: "During presentations, speakers will not engage in any type of promotional marketing or selling of any product or service." https://academyhealth.org/node/25153
- [data] (policy) HPA Tech Retreat: no "direct or implied sales pitches, promotional messaging... price lists, discount offers, 'contact sales,' or lead-gen calls to action", including in sponsored sessions. Note that this one bans lead-generation calls to action as well. https://hpaonline.com/hpa-tech-retreat-speaker-presentation-policy/
- [data] (policy) SNAME and OTC: "overt commercialism is prohibited outside of the commercial exhibit areas"; SNAME says repeat violators may be barred from future programs. https://sname.org/commercialism-policies , https://www.otcnet.org/conference-paper-policies
- [data] (policy) ATD requires education programs to be "noncommercial in content and delivery". One association's policy notes that sessions promoting a product the speaker provides "generally will not be approved for continuing education credit" by state licensing bodies. https://www.td.org/conference-policies
- [data] (policy) TEDx rules: events "may neither pay nor charge speakers"; speakers "cannot promote their own products, books, or businesses"; they "may never use the TED or TEDx stage to pitch their products or services, plug their books, or ask for funding". https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/before-you-start/tedx-rules , https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker/tedx-content-guidelines-details
- [data] (policy) Rotary clubs and chambers: no sales presentations (section 1a).
- [data] The planner survey above lists self-promotion from the stage among the top irritants.

**What is sometimes allowed**

- [data] (policy) ISMPP permits sponsored content if it stays "primarily educational" and "clearly disclose[s] the sponsoring organization at the beginning". A sponsored slot is a different product from a selected talk and should be labelled as such. https://www.ismpp.org/educational-content-policy

**Honest alternatives.** These are practice, not findings, and each depends on the host's written permission, because at least one published policy (HPA) bans lead-generation calls to action outright:

- [practitioner] A useful free resource tied to the talk (the checklist, the slides, a worksheet) offered with an opt-in, by QR code or short link, on the final slide.
- [practitioner] Ask the host beforehand, in writing, what is allowed: a closing slide with contact details, a handout, a sign-up sheet, staying after for questions, a follow-up session.
- [practitioner] Conversations after the talk and a follow-up to people who asked to be contacted.
- Legal note: anyone who opts in must have agreed to be emailed, and the emails are commercial messages under CAN-SPAM (section 8). A business card dropped in a bowl for a prize draw is not clear consent to a newsletter; say what people are signing up for.

**Data on conversion from a talk**

- UNCONFIRMED. No independent study was found of the share of an audience that becomes leads or clients. Everything in circulation is self-reported by coaches and vendors: "3 to 5 percent" raised to "30 to 50 percent", a funnel converting "10 to 25 percent" (a Buffer guest post), a "44 percent" case study from a software vendor, an "80 percent close rate" from one speaker's own discovery calls. These are [practitioner] claims with unknown samples. https://buffer.com/resources/speaking-sales-funnel
- [data] Weak and off-target: BigSpeak white paper contacted over 5,000 keynote buyers, 91 answered, up to 87 percent reported a return. This is buyer satisfaction with a tiny response rate, not audience conversion. https://www.bigspeak.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/White-Paper-_-What’s-the-Return-on-Investment-for-Event-Keynote-Speakers-4.pdf
- The skill must not quote any conversion rate. It can help the member measure their own: people in the room, opt-ins, conversations booked, clients.

## 6. Podcast guesting as a stage

- [data] Podchaser, State of Podcast Guest Pitching (2026), survey of producers and agencies managing over 100 podcasts: median acceptance rate 5 percent (about 1 pitch in 20); median response rate 17.5 percent; when a pitch is well matched to the show, about 70 percent of host responses are positive; producers report blacklisting whole email domains that send irrelevant, templated pitches. Only a staging URL and secondary write-ups were reachable; verify on podchaser.com. https://www.podchaser.com/articles/webinars/tactical-guide-to-podcast-guest-pitching
- [data] Podcast Hawk report (2025), cited second-hand: 71 percent of hosts give irrelevant pitches as the top reason for not replying. Original not seen. https://www.millionpodcasts.com/blog/do-podcasts-pay-their-guests/
- [data] Let's Make A Podcast (2026, 314 feeds): only 32 percent of shows show any public sign of booking outside guests, and 23 percent look dormant. Check a show is alive and takes guests before pitching. https://soundsprofitable.com/?p=12716
- [data] Interview Valet, 2026 State of Podcast Guesting (released 10 February 2026, from its own booking data): most interviews are recorded on Zoom (about 54 percent) or Riverside (about 30 percent). Full report is gated. https://offers.interviewvalet.com/sopg
- [practitioner] Pitch norms: show you have listened, name the audience benefit and two or three specific episode angles, keep it short. Hosts in a Listen Notes piece say standout pitches "prioritize the value they can bring to the audience". https://www.listennotes.com/podcast-academy/find-podcasts-to-be-a-guest-on-and-learn-whos-44
- UNCONFIRMED: "4.8 percent acceptance for cold pitches, 36 percent through referrals or platforms" and "top hosts get 42 pitches a week" (voxbooster.com). The cited surveys could not be found. Do not use.

**Pay-to-play**

- [data] Bloomberg's Ashley Carman reported (via WNYC On the Media, "Under the Table") that guests paying to appear on podcasts is an established practice, often undisclosed to listeners. https://wnycstudios.org/podcasts/otm/episodes/on-the-media-under-table
- [practitioner] One directory says most paid-guest requests come from small shows charging $200 to $2,000, and that over 99 percent of appearances involve no payment. Unsourced figures. https://www.millionpodcasts.com/blog/do-podcasts-pay-their-guests/
- Rule for the skill: guests are normally neither paid nor charged. A show that asks for money to appear is selling advertising; ask for download numbers in writing, and note that a paid appearance is a sponsored placement that should be disclosed to listeners under FTC endorsement principles.
- Booking agencies and matching sites are legitimate paid services, not scams, but prices move and are poorly documented (section 9).

## 7. Scams and bad deals aimed at aspiring speakers

- [data] (policy) FTC consumer alerts on business coaching: "How to spot and avoid business coaching scams" (April 2026) and "Not all business coaches are trying to help you" (September 2023). Warning signs: pressure to act now, guaranteed income, a "proven system". Advice: search the company name with "complaint", "scam" or "review"; report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/04/how-spot-and-avoid-business-coaching-scams , https://www.consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/09/not-all-business-coaches-are-trying-help-you
- [data] FTC cases: one coaching scheme charged up to $13,995, mostly on credit cards, for material largely free online; operators bought lead lists of people who had already been taken by other coaching offers; the MOBE case involved more than $300 million. Being taken once puts you on a list. https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2018/05/ftc-says-consumers-struck-out-deceptive-business-coaching-pitches , https://www.consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2020/03/ftc-goes-after-several-top-affiliates-mobe
- [data] FTC put more than 1,100 businesses on notice about deceptive money-making claims (October 2021). https://consumer.ftc.gov/blog/2021/10/ftc-puts-over-1100-businesses-notice-about-deceptive-money-making-claims
- [data] Vanity awards: BBB Scam Tracker has received about 1,300 reports since 2015 involving the word "award". Typical asks: $149 to $229 for a plaque, or $150 for a plaque and $1,500 for a "media package". Signs: an award you did not apply for, a fee to receive it. https://www.cbsnews.com/boston/news/cambridge-business-owner-warns-about-award-vanity-scam/
- [data] "Who's Who" schemes: AARP warns that legitimate directories "don't charge for inclusion". Older professionals are a target. https://www.aarp.org/money/scams-fraud/beware-the-whos-who-directory-scam/
- [data] Fake TEDx: a Khaleej Times sting found "Guaranteed TEDx Speaker Packages" sold for $10,000 to $25,000 for a fake Dubai event. TEDx rules forbid charging speakers at all, so any fee to speak at a "TEDx" is a red flag by TED's own rules. https://www.khaleejtimes.com/uae/fake-tedx-scam-khaleej-times-sting-operation
- [practitioner] The "you have been selected" email: flattering invitation to speak or receive an honour, perks listed first, a participation fee (one example $3,500) near the end. https://minterdial.com/2026/08/vanity-awards-in-the-age-of-ai-when-recognition-becomes-pay-to-play
- [practitioner] Fake overseas invitations (church and university names used): the speaker is asked for a "work permit" fee or bank details. Hundreds of US Christian leaders were targeted in one 2020 wave. Check by contacting the named organisation through its own published details. https://www.christianitytoday.com/news/2020/january/uk-church-conference-email-scam-big-things-speaker-invites.html , https://greenandprofitable.com/ugent-speakers-beware-the-middlesex-university-scam/
- [practitioner] Pay-to-speak is not always fraud. Some events sell stage time. The honest version is disclosed as a sponsored slot; "if you buy your speaking slot, say so". https://michaelgerharz.com/the-dishonesty-of-paid-speaking/
- [practitioner] Bureaus: reputable ones are paid by commission, not listing fees. An upfront fee to "join a bureau" is a paid directory, not representation. https://www.bigspeak.com/about/faq/speakers-bureaus-faqs/
- UNCONFIRMED: any FTC or state attorney general action specifically about pay-to-speak events, fake speakers bureaus or paid speaker directories. None found. The FTC material is about coaching and money-making schemes in general. Do not tell members "the FTC has warned about pay-to-speak events".
- UNCONFIRMED: evidence on whether paid speaker directories (SpeakerHub, eSpeakers, SpeakerMatch) produce bookings for new speakers. No independent data either way.

**Checks the skill can teach.** Did I apply? Who pays whom? Is the fee stated up front? Can I find last year's event, its venue and past speakers independently? Does the organiser's contact match the organisation's own website? Is there pressure to decide today? Is income or exposure "guaranteed"?

## 8. Law and rules

Not legal advice. The skill should tell members to read the linked source and take advice for anything with money or rights attached. US law unless stated.

**Cold email to organisers: CAN-SPAM**

- [data] (policy) FTC, "CAN-SPAM Act: A Compliance Guide for Business". The Act covers commercial email and, per the FTC, "makes no exception for business-to-business email". Core duties: no false or misleading header information; no deceptive subject lines; identify the message as an ad where it is one; include a valid physical postal address; tell recipients how to opt out; honour opt-outs within 10 business days; you remain responsible for anyone sending on your behalf. https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business
- [data] (policy) Penalty: up to $53,088 per email in violation (figure for penalties assessed after 17 January 2025; adjusted for inflation; reported second-hand). One source says the 2026 adjustment was cancelled and 2025 levels still apply: UNCONFIRMED. Point to the FTC page and do not hard-code the number.
- CAN-SPAM is an opt-out law: a one-to-one pitch to a program chair is lawful without prior consent if the duties above are met. A single personal pitch is still a commercial message if its primary purpose is to promote a service. Including a postal address and a plain "tell me if you'd rather not hear from me" line is the safe habit.

**Other countries differ**

- [practitioner] (law-firm and vendor summaries; regulator pages not read) Canada's CASL is consent-first: express or implied consent is needed before a commercial electronic message; a publicly published business address relevant to the recipient's role can support implied consent. Sources disagree on how long implied consent lasts (6 months or 2 years depending on type). https://mcmillan.ca/insights/key-differences-between-us-and-canadian-anti-spam-laws/
- [practitioner] UK PECR: corporate subscribers can be emailed without prior consent if you identify yourself and offer an opt-out; sole traders and some partnerships are treated as individuals and need consent. UK and EU GDPR also apply to a named person's business address; "legitimate interests" with a balancing test and opt-out is the usual basis.
- For the skill: if the organiser is outside the US, say the rules differ and send the member to the regulator (CRTC, ICO) before sending.

**Testimonials and results claims from the stage**

- [data] (policy) FTC Endorsement Guides, revised 29 June 2023. The old "results not typical" safe harbour is gone; if a testimonial shows results that are not what people can generally expect, the advertiser must clearly disclose the generally expected results. FTC research found even prominent disclaimers do not undo the impression that a testimonial is typical. https://www.steptoe.com/en/news-publications/ftc-unveils-new-endorsement-guides-and-proposed-rule-on-consumer-reviews-what-your-business-needs-to-know-and-opportunities-to-shape-whats-next.html
- [data] (policy) FTC Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465, announced 14 August 2024, effective 21 October 2024. Bans fake or false reviews and testimonials, buying reviews conditioned on sentiment, undisclosed insider testimonials and fake social proof; civil penalties for knowing violations (announced at up to $51,744 per violation, later adjusted). The FTC sent warning letters under the rule in late 2025. https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/08/ftc-issues-final-rule-on-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials , https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2025/12/ftc-signals-heightened-enforcement-of-new
- Applied to a talk: a client story told on stage or on a slide is a testimonial in advertising. It must be real, used with permission, not paid for without saying so, and not presented as typical unless it is. Earnings or results claims need a basis. Affiliate or commission relationships (a summit, a recommended tool) must be disclosed.
- The session cannot check whether a member's client result is true or typical. It must ask, and must not write the claim as fact on the member's behalf.

**Recording and release**

- [practitioner] (law-firm chart) US states split between one-party and all-party consent for recording conversations; a majority are one-party, roughly a dozen require all parties (lists differ between sources). This governs private conversations and calls more than a public talk, but it is a reason never to record a room or a call silently. https://ktslaw.com/insights/alert/2024/7/wiretap-laws-in-the-united-states
- [practitioner] Speaker agreements should state recording rights expressly: "if the agreement is silent on recording, assume there will be a dispute". Common split: the event may record and share with attendees; the speaker keeps ownership of the content; public posting needs separate permission. https://cagspeakers.com/what-should-keynote-speaker-contract-include/
- For a free local talk there is usually no contract. The member should ask in writing: will it be recorded, who may post it, may I have a copy, may I use it as a clip.
- UNCONFIRMED: authoritative guidance on right-of-publicity and speaker release forms. Not found.

**Slides and music copyright**

- [practitioner] Images: an image shown in a live room and the same image in slides or a recording posted online are different uses; posting is where demand letters arise (one cited case: a $750 demand over a PowerPoint; one association was drawn into a claim after a presenter's slides were uploaded). Safe sources: your own images, public domain, properly licensed stock, Creative Commons with attribution. Microsoft 365 stock images are licensed for use inside 365 apps and recordings made with them. https://www.fasken.com/en/knowledge/2024/04/copyright-and-presentations-how-to-avoid-presentation-related-copyright-infringement , https://www.copyrightlaws.com/legally-using-images-presentation-slides/ , https://www.tdwg.org/news/2022/copyright-infringement/
- [practitioner] Music: playing recorded music at a public event needs a public performance licence from the performing rights organisations (ASCAP, BMI and others). PROs look to the venue or organiser, and many venues hold blanket licences, but that should be checked and not assumed. A performance licence does not cover synchronising music to video or slides, so walk-on music caught in a posted recording is a separate problem. https://www.pcma.org/3-key-lessons-for-licensing-music-at-your-next-event/ , https://imexevents.com/events-industry-knowledge-hub/playing-music-at-events-and-exhibitions-getting-a-performing-rights-organization-license
- UNCONFIRMED: US Copyright Office guidance on fair use in recorded talks. Fair use is fact-specific; the skill should not tell a member a use "is fair use".

**Speaker agreements**

- [practitioner] Terms that recur in bureau and template guidance: fee and payment schedule; expenses and travel (who books, class, per diem, or a flat buyout); cancellation by either side, with tiered schedules (one sample: 60+ days deposit kept, 30 to 59 days 50 percent due, under 30 days full fee) and reciprocity; force majeure, mutual; deliverables and length; AV requirements; recording rights and where the recording may be used; intellectual property staying with the speaker; exclusivity (not speaking for a competitor or in the same market within a window); indemnity; conduct. https://cagspeakers.com/what-should-keynote-speaker-contract-include/ , https://gothamartists.com/navigating-keynote-speaker-contracts-what-to-look-for-before-you-book/ , https://nar.realtor/publications/realtor-ae-magazine/speaker-shock-contract-know-how
- UNCONFIRMED: an attorney-authored or NSA model agreement. Search returned only templates and bureau guides.
- For unpaid local talks, a confirmation email covering date, time, length, audience, AV, recording, what may be offered at the end and who to call on the day does the job of a contract.

## 10. Older first-time speakers

**Age and credibility**

- [data] Listener studies of voice give a mixed picture. A study of 24 speakers aged 22 to 79 found listeners judged older speakers less powerful and less engaged, but rated them more strongly for wisdom and as good storytellers. A UCL study found older talkers sounded less trustworthy and less attractive, with little effect of listener age. A 2025 study of Tyneside voices found ratings depend heavily on speaker age and gender, with middle-aged male voices rated highest on professionalism and intelligence. https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10173768/1/McGettigan_Investigating%20the%20effects%20of%20talker%20age%20and%20listener%20age%20on%20trait%20perception%20from%20adult%20voices.pdf , https://journals.sub.uni-hamburg.de/hup2/jlar/article/view/1507
- These are laboratory judgements of short voice clips by strangers. None tests a live talk by a practitioner with decades of experience to an audience that chose to attend.
- UNCONFIRMED: Truong and Weber (2022), "Trust Issues: The Effect of Speaker Age on Credibility" exists (University of Tübingen) but its findings were not retrievable. http://hdl.handle.net/10900/134546
- UNCONFIRMED: any study of older first-time business speakers, or of what audiences respond to from them. None found. Statements such as "audiences trust grey hair" or "older speakers must work harder to seem current" are [folklore] in both directions.
- What the evidence supports saying: the research is mixed, and the reported strengths are wisdom and storytelling. The planner data (section 3) says relevance and tailoring to the audience are what get speakers chosen and rebooked, and those do not depend on age.

**The audience's hearing (many local audiences are older too)**

- [data] NIDCD: disabling hearing loss affects about 5 percent of adults 45 to 54, 10 percent of those 55 to 64, 22 percent of those 65 to 74 and 55 percent of those 75 and older. In a Rotary or library audience a meaningful share of the room cannot hear an unamplified voice well. https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/statistics/quick-statistics-hearing
- Practical consequence: always use the microphone when one is offered, including for "I have a loud voice" speakers; repeat audience questions into the microphone.

**Accessibility basics**

- [data] (policy) W3C Web Accessibility Initiative, "Making Events Accessible: Checklist for meetings, conferences, training, and presentations" covers in-person, remote and hybrid events for presenters and organisers: adequate sound systems, good visibility and lighting of speakers and interpreters, materials shared in advance with interpreters and captioners, captions or transcripts for audio, description of visual information. The speaker-specific wording (use the microphone, large text, describe what is on the slide) is standard in this guidance but the exact text was not retrievable. UNCONFIRMED wording; read the page. https://www.w3.org/WAI/teach-advocate/accessible-presentations/
- [practitioner] For the speaker's own needs, ask the host ahead: a lectern or table for notes, a confidence monitor or printed notes in large type, a chair or stool, a handheld or clip-on microphone, steps or a ramp to any stage, lighting good enough to read by, a glass of water, and a run-through of the clicker. No source needed for these to be reasonable requests; no data found on them.
- UNCONFIRMED: research on large-type notes or font sizes for older presenters specifically. Not found.

## 11. Ten pieces of speaking-business folklore the evidence does not support

1. **"93 percent of communication is non-verbal (7-38-55)."** [folklore] Mehrabian's two small studies concerned feelings conveyed by single words with conflicting tone; nothing in them applies to speeches. Words matter. https://bigthink.com/the-learning-curve/the-7-38-55-rule-debunking-the-golden-ratio-of-conversation , https://ubiquity.acm.org/article.cfm?id=3289281
2. **"People fear public speaking more than death."** [folklore] Traced to a 1973 Bruskin survey that asked people to pick fears from a list, not to rank them against dying. [data] Chapman University Survey of American Fears: public speaking at 28.9 percent afraid or very afraid in the 2026 wave, 33.7 percent in 2025, 28.7 percent in 2023, well down the list and below fears such as dying in the wave reported second-hand. https://www.chapman.edu/wilkinson/research-centers/babbie-center/_files/2025/2026-Fear-Study-Complete-List-of-Fears-ABC-order.pdf , https://prorhetoric.com/another-new-study-advances-another-old-canard-about-public-speaking/
3. **"Audiences have an 8-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish."** [folklore] Comes from a 2015 Microsoft marketing report whose figure has no traceable research behind it; the BBC found attention is task-dependent and an average span is not meaningful. https://internet.psych.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/532-Master/532-UnitPages/Unit-09/Goldfish_MythBusting.pdf
4. **"You need a professional sizzle reel before anyone will book you."** [folklore] for local and association breakout stages. Published speaker forms ask for topic, description, takeaways and bio (section 1). Bureaus and paid keynote buyers do want video. No survey gives a figure.
5. **"Speak for free and the paid bookings will follow."** [folklore] as an automatic rule. No data links free talks to later fees. Association breakouts stay unpaid by policy however good the speaker is; AAE 2024 found nearly two-thirds of experienced speakers say finding engagements is their biggest challenge.
6. **"Get on a speakers bureau and they will find you work."** Contradicted. Bureaus say they take speakers who already have demand; one wants $300,000 in bookings before exclusive representation (section 1d).
7. **"Typical fees: beginners $500 to $2,500, professionals $5,000 to $10,000..."** [folklore] Such tables carry no source. The only surveys sample established speakers on vendor lists (section 2). Chambers, Rotary, most association breakouts and TEDx pay nothing by policy.
8. **"Sell from the stage: a good close converts 10 to 30 percent of the room."** [folklore] No independent conversion data exists (section 5), and most stages open to a new speaker forbid selling in writing.
9. **"A TEDx talk will launch your speaking business" / "you can buy a TEDx slot."** TEDx rules forbid paying or charging speakers and forbid promoting a business from the stage; paid "guaranteed TEDx" packages have been exposed as fake (sections 5 and 7). No data found on bookings resulting from TEDx talks. UNCONFIRMED either way on career effect.
10. **"Start with small podcasts; big shows won't take a cold pitch" and "just send more pitches."** Contradicted or unsupported. The one feed study found larger independent shows more approachable than small ones, with a third of shows showing any sign of taking guests; Podchaser reports a median 5 percent acceptance and producers blacklisting domains that send templated pitches (section 6). Volume without fit is punished.

Also unsupported, for the reserve list: "people remember 10 percent of what they hear and 90 percent of what they do" (the figures are not in Edgar Dale's Cone of Experience; this session's search did not retrieve the debunking source, so UNCONFIRMED here); "tailor your talk to visual, auditory and kinesthetic learners" (learning-styles matching lacks support in the research literature; source not retrieved this session, UNCONFIRMED here); "the CAN-SPAM Act doesn't apply to B2B email" (false, per the FTC, section 8); "add 'results not typical' and you are covered" (false since the 2023 Endorsement Guides, section 8).

## Honest limits: what an AI session cannot know or verify

The session **cannot know**, and must never state as fact:

- An event's real audience size, attendance, demographics, or whether its attendees are the member's buyers. Organisers' own figures are claims.
- Whether an event, club, podcast or summit still exists, is still meeting, or is taking speakers this year. A third of podcast feeds in one study showed no sign of booking guests and nearly a quarter were dormant.
- Whether a named contact (program chair, producer) is still in the role, or whether an email address is current. Volunteer roles turn over yearly.
- Whether a call-for-speakers deadline is right. Aggregators mark their own deadlines unverified; only the host's page is authoritative, and the session may not be able to open it.
- Whether a fee quote is typical, fair or negotiable. No reliable fee data exists for this tier.
- Whether a host pays, covers travel, records, shares the attendee list or allows an offer. Only the host can say, in writing.
- A club's, chamber's or association's rules on selling. They differ host to host.
- Reply rates, acceptance rates or conversion rates the member should "expect". No speaking-specific data exists; the member's own numbers are the only real ones.
- Whether an invitation, award, directory, bureau or "selected speaker" offer is legitimate. The session can list warning signs and checks; it cannot clear an offer as safe.
- Whether a vendor's price, plan or free tier is current. Several conflicting prices were found on the same day.
- Whether a member's client result or testimonial is true, typical or permitted to be used.
- Whether an image, clip or song is licensed for the member's use, or whether a use is "fair use".
- What the law requires in the member's state or country for their facts. It can point to the FTC, CRTC or ICO page and say when to ask a lawyer.
- How an audience will respond to this speaker because of age, accent, gender or style.

The session **can**: help find and read a host's published guidelines when the member pastes them in; draft a pitch to the host's stated criteria; build a one-sheet from true facts the member supplies; prepare the questions to ask a host in writing; keep a log of pitches and outcomes so the member builds their own reply and conversion numbers; flag scam patterns; and mark every figure it uses with where it came from and its date.
