# Own two topics, talk to the right people, write in your own words

A solo expert wins clients from organic LinkedIn in late 2026 by being clearly about two or three topics, by holding real conversations with a small number of the right people, and by writing from their own experience. Reach is not the lever. In March 2026 LinkedIn rebuilt its feed around language-model retrieval and a transformer "Generative Recommender" that matches posts to members by what a post is about ([LinkedIn Engineering](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/feed/engineering-the-next-generation-of-linkedins-feed); [LinkedIn](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/ImprovingTheFeed)). Van der Blom's 2026 data, relayed secondhand, puts reach for active creators down about **60% over two years** and network-based distribution at about **10%**, from 70% in 2024 ([Melanie Goodman](https://melaniegoodmanlinkedinconsultant.substack.com/p/linkedin-algorithm-2026-reach-topic-authority)). The median personal-profile post gets about **860 impressions** ([AuthoredUp](https://authoredup.com/blog/linkedin-metrics-to-track)). Conversation moved the other way: LinkedIn's VP of Product reports feed comments **up 24%** a year and says **2 to 5 posts a week** is enough ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-shares-tips-on-how-to-boost-your-in-app-performance-in-2026/808448/)). LinkedIn names pods, comment automation and bait as targets, says it identifies generic content correctly **94% of the time** in testing ([LinkedIn](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/keeping-conversations-real-on-linkedin)), and its product chief says posts flagged as "AI slop" get about **40% fewer views** ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/07/31/linkedin-seems-like-ai-slop-button-billions-automated-comments-attempts/)). "Comment KEYWORD for the guide" posts fell to about **half their author's usual reach** from late July 2026 in one vendor study of 684,000 posts ([MagicPost](https://magicpost.in/studies/linkedin-comment-bait)). Warm signals beat volume. LinkedIn's own 2022 InMail data shows acceptance **45% to 78% higher** when the prospect recently posted, changed jobs or had their profile viewed first ([LinkedIn Sales Blog](https://www.linkedin.com/business/sales/blog/prospecting/data-on-sending-inmails-higher-acceptance-rates)). Five vendor datasets agree a connection note does not raise acceptance, though it lifts replies from 5.3% to **8.2%** ([Belkins](https://belkins.io/blog/linkedin-outreach-study)), and three-message sequences beat five or more, **9.8% against 5.0%** ([Expandi](https://expandi.io/state-of-linkedin-outreach-h2-2026/)). LinkedIn's rules leave no room for software to act inside LinkedIn at any volume ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1341387)). The design that follows is a profile rebuilt as a landing page, a voice-first system producing three posts a week, about 20 minutes a day of hand-written comments and warm-signal messages, two follow-ups and then stop, and a weekly scorecard from LinkedIn's export and the member's own log. It runs on two clocks: warm outbound in weeks, content inbound in months.

## Almost none of LinkedIn's own pages were read in full

The research environment could not open linkedin.com, engineering.linkedin.com or learn.microsoft.com. All five research streams report the same block: fetches failed on DNS and the proxy refused LinkedIn and Microsoft hosts. Every claim attributed here to LinkedIn therefore rests on **search-engine extracts of LinkedIn pages and on secondary coverage quoting them**. That includes the March 2026 engineering post, the 2026 newsroom posts, the Help Center, the User Agreement and the developer documentation. Quoted phrases are the wording the search tool showed, not wording checked against the live page. Richard van der Blom's *Algorithm Insights* reports are paywalled, so his figures come from podcasts and recaps, which disagree on samples and percentages. Two public GitHub repositories were the only sources read in full, and they are practitioner work.

Strength is marked in words. "LinkedIn says" means a LinkedIn page or executive on the record, seen as an extract. "LinkedIn via press" means a LinkedIn statement reported by trade press. "Data" names a third-party dataset and sample. "Practitioner" means one named person's method. "Folklore" means a repeated claim with no traceable source. Where the notes conflict, the newest gap-filling stream (16 October 2026) wins on the facts it covers, and the text says so where it matters.

These are the claims most worth checking on a primary page before they reach members.

A second limit matters as much. Most of the numbers come from **vendors who sell LinkedIn tools**. AuthoredUp, Ordinal, LigoSocial, MagicPost and Metricool sell content or analytics software. Expandi, Belkins, Waalaxy and La Growth Machine sell outreach, much of it automated, so their benchmarks describe cold campaigns and are a floor for warm, hand-written work. Their content data describe creators in general, not 45–65 service experts. And **no large-sample study exists of how long a solo expert takes to win a first client from organic LinkedIn**. Every timeline here is practitioner consensus or inference.

## The feed now matches posts to people by topic

The March 2026 rebuild is the most important fact in this report. The engineering post by Hristo Danchev, dated 12 March 2026, is described by secondary sources as replacing "a fragmented set of independent retrieval pipelines" with one LLM-based retrieval model. Ranking moved to a sequential transformer, the Generative Recommender, that reads a member's feed history as an ordered sequence (one summary says 1,000+ interactions), runs on GPUs and updates embeddings "within minutes" ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/linkedin-rebuilds-its-feed-from-scratch-with-llms-and-gpu-powered-ranking/)). Search Engine Land reports that it "prioritizes topical relevance and engagement patterns, LinkedIn said" ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/linkedin-updates-feed-algorithm-llm-ranking-retrieval-471708)). That is LinkedIn via press. The newsroom post is LinkedIn's own word, as an extract. It describes models "known as Generative Recommenders, augmented with large language models (LLMs)", says LinkedIn is "working to make engagement pods ineffective and curb comment automation", and says it is cutting recycled posts and bait ([LinkedIn](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/ImprovingTheFeed)). The Help Center adds that the feed uses a member's profile, what they view and how they interact, and includes posts from outside their network ([LinkedIn Help](https://linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1339724)).

LinkedIn researchers have also published papers, including 360Brew, a 150B-parameter model ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16450)). Papers are not proof of production. Many blogs call 360Brew "the feed algorithm", but LinkedIn has never confirmed it, and the March 2026 post does not use the name ([La Réclame](https://lareclame.fr/linkedin-360brew-325321)). The confirmed facts are the LLM retrieval, the Generative Recommender and the topic understanding.

What this means in practice comes from van der Blom, not LinkedIn. His 2026 data show connection-based distribution falling from 70% (2024) to 54% (2025) to 10% (2026), while interest-based distribution rose from 5% to 50%. Sticking to **2 to 3 core topics for 90 days** goes with about **27% more reach and 41% more topic-based follows**. These are data claims, secondhand, with the method hidden. They point the same way as LinkedIn's statement that the model matches posts to professional interests, so the rule is safe even if the numbers are not: **pick two or three topics tied to the offer and hold them for a quarter.** Van der Blom adds, as a practitioner claim, that commenting on off-topic posts can hurt, because comments shape the commenter's own interest profile ([Mics to Millions](https://micstomillions.podbean.com/e/boost-visibility-increase-engagement-and-secure-leads-on-linkedin-with-richard-van-der-blom-part-2-ep-61)).

Dwell time is real; its multipliers are not. LinkedIn's 2020 engineering post defines feed dwell time ([LinkedIn Engineering](https://engineering.linkedin.com/blog/2020/understanding-feed-dwell-time)), and the 2026 ranker reportedly uses it. Blog figures like "X seconds gets Y% reach" are folklore. Hashtags no longer help: LinkedIn told Social Media Today that its algorithms "now consider a broader range of contextual cues" ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/do-you-need-to-use-hashtags-on-linkedin/704535/)).

Reach fell as conversation rose. Van der Blom's 2026 edition (about 1.3M posts) reports reach down about 60% over two years ([Creator Science](https://podcast.creatorscience.com/richard-van-der-blom-2/)); his 2025 edition reported −47% or −50%, depending on the recap. AuthoredUp's median is 860 impressions per post (top 5%: 8,400). Meanwhile Microsoft said in July 2025 that comments "rose over 30%", and in October 2025 LinkedIn's CEO cited comments up 24% ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-reports-increase-in-post-comments-video-posts-microsoft-q1-2026/804353/)). From about August 2026, LinkedIn orders comments for each viewer by "professional interests, connections and engagement activity" and surfaces more live discussions, citing time in comments up 18% ([Social Media Today](https://socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-updates-feed-display-to-drive-more-post-replies-and-comments/827394)). An earlier note called this a verified announcement. The newer note found only that one trade source, so this report treats it as **LinkedIn via press**. Some comment growth is automated: van der Blom says about 80% of early comments on his own posts are AI-written. The research team's interpretation is that supply has outgrown attention and distribution has narrowed. For a solo expert, **800 impressions from the right 800 people is a good post.**

Post from the personal profile. Metricool's 2026 study of 673,658 posts finds personal profiles get a **63% higher engagement rate** than company pages ([Metricool](https://metricool.com/press-release-linkedin-study-2026/)), and Ordinal finds a typical company-page post reaches about **1.6% of followers** ([Ordinal](https://www.tryordinal.com/blog/the-declining-reach-of-linkedin-company-pages)).

## Documents and images earn reach, video earns trust, and three posts a week is enough

The best format data for personal profiles is AuthoredUp's 3M+ posts from March 2025 to February 2026, each format compared with the author's own average ([AuthoredUp](https://authoredup.com/blog/best-performing-content-on-linkedin)). All of it is data from a vendor.

| Format | Reach | Engagement | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Document (PDF carousel) | **1.39x** | **1.30x** | Only 4.9% of posts; tops Socialinsider's company-page data too |
| Single image | **1.20x** | **1.33x** | 57% of posts, 65% of saves |
| Text only | 1.07x | **0.78x** | |
| Native video | **0.86x** | n/a | Median video reach −36% a year; the only major format below average |
| Poll | **1.78x** | **0.37x** | Reach without engagement |

Some recaps credit the same multipliers to van der Blom's 2025 report. Another AuthoredUp blog citing van der Blom 2025 gives polls 1.64x, documents 1.45x and images 1.18x. All 2025–26 datasets put documents and images near the top. Van der Blom's practitioner caution is that three carousels in a row start to reduce reach.

Video is where LinkedIn's push and measured results part. LinkedIn reports video uploads and watch time each up 36% a year as of early 2025 ([LinkedIn](https://news.linkedin.com/2025/Q3FY25_Earnings_Highlights)), offers a vertical player on mobile only ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a6290168)), and says it is cutting "videos that don't match the post text". Independent 2026 data disagree. Shield's March index of about 50,000 posts puts median video reach rate at 7% against 12% for text ([Shield](https://theshieldindex.beehiiv.com/p/march-2026-linkedin-reach-by-content-format)). Ordinal's 215,824 posts average 7,995 impressions for text and 6,830 for video ([Ordinal](https://www.tryordinal.com/blog/carousel-posts)). For a 45–65 expert, video's job is trust with people who already follow.

The other formats each have one narrow job.

| Format | Evidence | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Poll | LinkedIn said in 2022 it shows fewer polls from strangers ([LinkedIn](https://blog.linkedin.com/2022/may/5/keeping-your-feed-relevant-and-productive)) | At most monthly, as research |
| Article | Median reach 596 against 921 for text ([AuthoredUp](https://authoredup.com/blog/linkedin-articles-vs-posts)); LinkedIn via press says articles drive AI-chatbot citations ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-shares-tips-on-optimizing-post-content-for-ai-chatbots/814635/)) | A home for a long piece |
| Newsletter | LinkedIn invites connections and followers only "once the first article of your newsletter has been successfully published" ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/97507)) | Start at about 500 followers (John Espirian, practitioner) |
| LinkedIn Live | Must be scheduled as an Event from 22 June 2026, LinkedIn via press ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-will-no-longer-allow-real-time-livestreams/816050/)) | Optional; not in the first 90 days |
| Repost | No LinkedIn data | A substantive comment on the original beats a plain repost |

Links are disputed. A LinkedIn product director said in September 2025 there is no penalty for link posts that lead with value, as relayed by Matt Navarra ([Threads](https://www.threads.com/@mattnavarra/post/DOWa_61Cown/)). Van der Blom measures **−18.8% median reach** for one link in the body. Metricool's 2025 study of about 577,000 posts found link posts got **13.57% more interactions** ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/linkedin-study-finds-adding-links-boosts-engagement-by-13/544524/)). MagicPost finds link cards roughly halve impressions ([MagicPost](https://magicpost.in/blog/linkedin-external-links-reach)). A Forbes "60% link penalty" has contested methods ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jodiecook/2026/07/30/the-linkedin-link-penalty-cutting-your-reach-by-60/)). The studies measure different things. Make most posts complete without a link, and test body against first comment when a link is the point.

Cadence has good data. Buffer's 2M+ posts from 94k+ accounts show that moving from one post a week to **2 to 5 adds about 1,182 impressions per post**, with diminishing gains above ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/how-often-to-post-on-linkedin/)). Sachdeva, via press, says "aim to publish between 2-5 LinkedIn posts per week" and "posting more than once a day won't hurt your total reach". Van der Blom's 2023 rule of 18 hours between posts conflicts on its face, but it measured per-post growth while Sachdeva speaks of total reach, so the newer note holds both can be true ([Malay Mail](https://www.malaymail.com/news/life/2023/05/11/what-and-when-should-you-post-on-linkedin/68608)). Short breaks do not seem to hurt: Ordinal found weekly posters and accounts with two-week gaps at 1.85% and 1.89% median engagement, a directional result ([Ordinal](https://www.tryordinal.com/blog/linkedin-content-strategy)). Vulse finds accounts posting three or more times a week grew impressions 32% over a year, a correlation ([Vulse](https://vulse.co/blog/linkedin-consistency-data-700-million-impressions)). **Three posts a week, every week, is the defensible default.**

Timing is a test. An earlier note said all major studies agree on Tuesday to Thursday. The newer note shows they do not.

| Study | Best times |
|---|---|
| Buffer 2026, 4.8M posts ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/best-time-to-post-on-linkedin/)) | 3 pm to 8 pm; Wed > Thu > Fri; Mon and Tue weakest |
| Sprout Social 2026, 307k profiles ([Sprout](https://sproutsocial.com/insights/best-times-to-post-on-linkedin/)) | Tue to Thu, roughly 11 am to 5 pm |
| Hootsuite ([Hootsuite](https://blog.hootsuite.com/best-time-to-post-on-linkedin/)) | Tue and Wed early morning; its own pages disagree on the hour |

Start midweek in the buyer's hours, then compare the member's own medians over four weeks. The "golden hour" has no LinkedIn source. Metricool finds **half of a post's impressions arrive within 48 hours** ([Metricool](https://newsletter.metricool.com/p/5-linkedin-stats-in-2026-you-probably-didn-t-see-coming)). Fixing a typo after posting is fine; deleting and reposting to "reset" is not.

## The first 140 characters carry the post, and the whole idea carries the rest

LinkedIn publishes no "see more" cut-off. Practitioners plan on about **140 characters on mobile and 210 on desktop**, roughly three short lines, and line breaks pull the fold earlier. Jasmin Alić teaches focusing on "the first three lines of text, including spaces" ([Rosetta](https://rosetta.to/person/jasmin-alic)). One inference is worth stating: because the model reads the text to decide what a post is about, the first lines should **name the topic and the reader in the buyer's words**. That follows from the verified architecture but has not been measured.

On length, three large datasets agree that a whole idea beats a middling one.

| Study | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| AuthoredUp ([AuthoredUp](https://authoredup.com/blog/linkedin-character-limit)) | 372,126 posts | Peaks at 1,301 to 2,000 characters (2.61%) and 2,001 to 2,500 (2.67%) |
| AuthoredUp, newer (same page) | 709,195 posts | By format: image captions 800 to 1,000, carousels 800 to 1,000; gain only 3% to 5% |
| Ordinal ([Ordinal](https://www.tryordinal.com/blog/linkedin-post-size)) | 248,275 posts | Peaks at 2,000 to 3,000 (1.33%); weakest at 600 to 1,500 |
| LigoSocial ([LigoSocial](https://ligosocial.com/research/linkedin-post-length)) | 134,522, within-author | 2,400+ characters about 24% above 600 to 900; under 150 characters −31% |

An earlier note cited an "800 to 1,000 character sweet spot". The newer AuthoredUp data show that is a small, per-format effect for image and carousel captions. For text posts the default is **about 1,300 to 2,500 characters**.

For openings, AuthoredUp's study of 309,614 posts (December 2025 to May 2026), reported by Ordinal, ranks median engagement: **story 2.60%**, contrarian 2.31%, statement 2.27%, results 2.19%, **question 2.16%, last** ([Ordinal](https://www.tryordinal.com/blog/linkedin-hooks)). Earlier write-ups gave the sample as 309k to 622k; the newer figure is used. White space helps: in one Stan Store analysis of 800 to 1,200 character posts, 41+ line breaks reached 0.675% engagement against 0.147% for 1 to 5, sample not stated ([Stan Store](https://stan.store/blog/linkedin-engagement/)). AuthoredUp's 843,470 posts show 0 to 1 emoji goes with 22% more median reach, flattening after two ([AuthoredUp](https://authoredup.com/blog/linkedin-text-formatting)). **Open with a specific scene, result or claim inside 140 characters, not a question, then deliver the whole idea in short paragraphs.**

## Generic AI text is now a measured reach risk

LinkedIn allows AI-assisted writing. Its Help page says: "We urge you to review, edit, and approve of any content that you've used AI to create. You are ultimately responsible for everything you post on LinkedIn." It suggests disclosure when AI played a major role and defines "AI slop" as content that looks polished "but lacks a clear point of view or substance" ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1481496)). In May 2026 LinkedIn's Laura Lorenzetti wrote that "we're correctly identifying generic content 94% of the time", that AI-looking content without a point of view will be "less likely to be widely distributed beyond a person's immediate network", and that AI is fine if posts "represent your voice and your perspectives" ([LinkedIn](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/keeping-conversations-real-on-linkedin)). An earlier note credited the 94% to coverage of the slop button. The newer note traces it to Lorenzetti's post, which is used here.

On 30 July 2026 LinkedIn added a "Seems like AI slop" option to posts ([404 Media](https://404media.co/linkedin-introduces-a-seems-like-ai-slop-button)). CPO Hari Srinivasan, via Fortune, said the flags train "a series of new and improved classifiers" and that slop gets about 40% fewer views. More than a million people clicked it within weeks ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/08/25/1-million-people-clicked-linkedin-ai-slop/)). LinkedIn also replaced its "Enhance your post" writer with a proofreader that keeps the author's voice ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-ditches-post-enhancement-launches-post-proofreader/830851/)). For images and video, the Help page on Content Credentials says "it's not yet possible to identify and label all AI-generated and modified content" ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a6282984)). The rules note could not find that page; the newer note did.

Third-party data agree in direction. Originality.ai's 2024 analysis of 8,795 long posts found likely-AI posts got 45% less engagement. Its 2025 study of 3,368 posts split by industry: AI did better in leadership and inspiration, human posts won by 44% in healthcare and 40% in government ([Originality.ai](https://originality.ai/blog/linkedin-ai-study-engagement)). The rules note credits the 45% to the 2025 study; this report follows the profile note's more detailed account. LigoSocial's within-author study of 134,522 posts, the strongest design, found posts that read as AI got **22% less engagement** than the same author's other posts ([LigoSocial](https://ligosocial.com/research/ai-linkedin-posts)). "AI posts get 2.8x less reach" has no source. The synthesis is that **the penalty falls on generic text, not on AI-assisted posts carrying real specifics**, and professional services sit closer to healthcare than to inspiration. For a 45–65 expert, decades of specific stories and earned judgment are what a model cannot produce. That is the moat.

## The profile is a landing page that the model also reads

Every comment, post and message sends people to the profile, and the ranking model reads it too. LinkedIn confirms only that the ranker models "what a post is about and how it relates to a member's evolving interests and career goals" ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-updates-its-feed-algorithm/814638/)). Keeping the headline, About section and pillars on one topic is a sensible hedge, not a proven factor.

|---|---|---|
| Creator Mode | Retired February to March 2024; its tools went to everyone ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedins-removing-its-creator-mode-option/706585/)). One source says early 2025; that is wrong | LinkedIn via press |
| Follow or Connect | Settings & Privacy > Visibility > Followers > "Make follow primary" ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a528011)) | LinkedIn says |

| Verification | "Up to 60% more profile views" and "up to 50% more engagement on posts" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/12/18/linkedins-profile-verification-push-is-accelerating-and-india-is-leading-the-charge-in-2025)) | LinkedIn's own ceiling figures |

The button and the Services section are mutually exclusive, which guides rarely mention. A member whose inbound comes from search should keep the free Services page and put the booking link in Featured. A member whose inbound comes from content should use the button, if they already pay for Premium Business. "Turn on Creator Mode" and "add profile hashtags" are obsolete.

The element guidance is practitioner method. Justin Welsh's headline test asks who specifically, what specifically and what outcome ([Welsh](https://www.justinwelsh.me/article/how-to-make-a-stand-out-linkedin-profille)). He treats the banner as prime real estate with one goal and one call to action, and limits Featured to one or two destinations, because featuring popular posts "keeps people scrolling without ever joining a list" ([Welsh](https://www.justinwelsh.me/newsletter/optimize-your-linkedin-for-2024)). The About structure in the notes is a synthesis: a two-line hook in the buyer's words, who the expert serves and who not, the problem reframed, the named method, three to five proof bullets with numbers, a human paragraph and one call to action. It leans on Edelman's 2025 finding that 85% of hidden buyers value "demonstrated understanding of their specific challenges" ([Edelman](https://www.edelman.com/news-awards/new-research-edelman-linkedin-thought-leadership-influences-b2b-sales)). On Follow or Connect, the notes infer that an expert with under about 5,000 connections whose goal is conversation should keep Connect, because first-degree connections can be messaged without InMail credits.

## A busy expert needs a capture system, not a writing habit

The audience is there: Pew's 2023 survey found 31% of US adults aged 50 to 64 use LinkedIn, against 40% of those aged 30 to 49. Most buyers are not ready. The 95:5 rule estimates only about 5% of B2B buyers are in market at once, so most content builds memory ([Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/peter-weinberg-jon-lombardo-95-5-rule)). Edelman and LinkedIn report 91% of hidden buyers say strong thought leadership makes them more receptive to outreach. Both studies have commercial sponsors.

The named practitioners teach overlapping systems, mostly read through secondary summaries.

| Jasmin Alić | First three lines; "You don't need to sell in your content to sell from it"; substantial comments | Quote page and webinar summary ([Social Media Tea](https://socialmediatea.beehiiv.com/p/get-100-extra-comments-linkedin)) |
| Richard van der Blom | 2 to 4 posts a week; 25 to 30 minutes of commenting a day; topic authority | Podcasts |
| Matt Barker | Problem, Agitate, Solution from researched pain | Podcast ([Copywriter Club](https://thecopywriterclub.com/linkedin-matt-barker/)) |
| Chris Donnelly | A free resource in nearly every post, with a one-word comment CTA | Third-party analysis ([Favikon](https://www.favikon.com/blog/who-is-chris-donnelly)) |
| Sahil Bloom | Curiosity hooks; named mental models with simple visuals | Third-party guides ([beehiiv](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/how-to-turn-any-newsletter-into-a-viral-x-thread)) |
| Dan Koe | Newsletter as the core asset, reused across platforms | Case study ([beehiiv](https://www.beehiiv.com/case-studies/dankoe)) |
| Alex Hormozi | Hook, Retain, Reward; give the method away, charge for implementation | Book contents via a listing ([Booxworm](https://booxworm.lk/products/100m-leads-alex-hormozi)) |

They agree on the opening (the first one to three lines decide the post), on volume (2 to 5 a week, though Donnelly and Alić post near daily) and on commenting as a growth engine. They disagree on story against teaching (Acosta and Bloom lead with story, which AuthoredUp's hook data supports; Welsh, Donnelly and Hormozi lean on frameworks) and on selling (Alić and Hormozi sell from content, Donnelly sells in nearly every post). Donnelly's one-word comment CTA is the pattern LinkedIn now suppresses, so that part should not be copied.

For a busy 45–65 expert, the notes' synthesis is capture before writing. Three pillars: **expertise and point of view**, **proof** (client stories with numbers), and **personal and values**, with an optional fourth for offers. Three posts a week: teaching, proof or story, and personal or soft-sell, with an explicit offer every second to fourth week. One 60 to 90 minute weekly session in which the expert answers three prompts by voice note, the answers are transcribed and shaped into drafts in the expert's own words, and the expert edits them. This mirrors LinkedIn's own move from an AI writer to a proofreader. No study measures how often to sell. The defensible default is a soft call to action in most posts, an explicit offer every two to four weeks, and a profile that sells the rest of the time. Proof posts are the practitioner-consensus bridge to buying, though no dataset isolates them.

## Conversations, not posts, produce clients

Commenting is the cheapest attention available. LinkedIn said, as reported by Lindsey Gamble, that commenting at least weekly can roughly triple profile views; the original post was not found ([Lindsey Gamble](https://www.lindseygamble.com/blog/linkedin-shares-commenting-can-triple-profile-views)). LinkedIn's Help warns that "if we detect excessive comment creation or use of an automation tool, we may limit the visibility of those comments" ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a524166)). Welsh recommends "thoughtful comments on five to ten niche posts a day", built from a specific compliment, an added insight and a question. The notes turn this into a three-tier watch list: 10 to 20 ideal clients, 10 to 20 peer experts whose audience is the buyer, and 3 to 5 larger voices. Length thresholds (5, 10 or 15 words "counting double") have no method, so the notes' rule of two to four sentences with one specific idea is a Build Room rule. Never pitch in comments. A commenter on the expert's own post who fits the ideal client is a warm signal.

Connection requests are capped, and notes are overrated. LinkedIn publishes no weekly number. Its Help says the restriction lasts a week and withdrawing invitations does not lift it ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a550555)). About 100 a week is the common report. On notes, the vendor evidence is unusually consistent.

| Study | Acceptance with note | Without | Replies with note | Without |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Belkins 2026 | 25.3% | 27.6% | 8.2% | 5.3% |
| Belkins 2025 (20M+ attempts) | 26.42% | 26.37% | 9.36% | 5.44% |
| La Growth Machine, ~27k leads ([LGM](https://lagrowthmachine.com/should-i-add-a-note-to-my-linkedin-connection-request/)) | 29% to 32% | 31% | n/a | n/a |
| Waalaxy 2025 | 32.3% | 34.3% | n/a | n/a |

Five datasets, counting an Expandi A/B test with a 1.28-point gap, agree a note does not raise acceptance. Two show it lifts replies. The "45% against 15%" claim has no source. Free notes are scarce: the Help copy seen says five a month at 200 characters ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a563153)), but it is old and 2026 reports say three. So blank requests are the default, with notes saved for clear signals. Belkins finds 91% of eventual accepters accept within two weeks ([Belkins](https://belkins.io/blog/linkedin-response-time-study)), which supports withdrawing requests pending after three.

Warm signals are the strongest LinkedIn-sourced conversion evidence. LinkedIn's 2022 InMail analysis found acceptance higher when the prospect follows the sender's company (+270%), when the sender viewed the prospect's profile within 30 days (+78%; another LinkedIn post says +86%), after a new job within 90 days (+62%), and when the prospect posted recently or shares a connection (+45% each). These are correlational and the method is unpublished. LinkedIn Talent data add that InMails **under 400 characters get about 22% more responses** ([LinkedIn Talent Blog](https://www.linkedin.com/business/talent/blog/talent-strategy/these-inmails-get-best-response-rates)). The usable signals for a solo expert are: commented on the expert's post, commented on a peer's post about the problem, viewed the profile and fits, new role, posted about the problem, sent a request, messaged first, shared an event.

The message frameworks are practitioner methods. Welsh sends 10 to 12 short, low-pressure messages every Friday, triggered by events in the client's world. Josh Braun teaches "poke the bear", a neutral question that surfaces a hidden problem, inside a four-part structure: Trigger, Think, Third-party, Talk. He stresses "genuine permission to say no" ([11x](https://www.11x.ai/guides/josh-braun-cold-outreach-method)). Morgan J. Ingram favours blank requests and "sell the meeting, not the product" ([Antoine Buteau](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-morgan-ingram/)). Lavender's "25 to 50 words get 65% more replies" comes from **email**, not LinkedIn, though Social Media Today presented it under an InMail heading ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-shares-inmail-best-practices-to-help-maximize-your-response-rates/632108/)). Matt Barker has no documented DM method. Together these support a short first message about the recipient, citing the signal, with one easy question, and a call ask only after two real exchanges.

Follow-up carries replies, but more is not better. In Belkins' 2026 campaigns replies split about evenly between first touch and follow-ups. Expandi's 2,966 campaigns show three messages replying at 9.8% against 5.0% for five or more. The notes' rule: first message within 24 to 48 hours of the signal, a value-adding follow-up after three to five days, a permission-to-close note after seven to ten, then stop. Voice notes are real but unmeasured. LinkedIn's Help says they are mobile-only, first-degree only, up to a minute ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a548233)). The only evidence is an anecdote of 125 voice messages producing 50 replies ([Barrows](https://blog.jbarrows.com/blog/linkedin-voice-messages/)). Use them as a warm second touch, never cold, never cloned.

Retire "comment KEYWORD for the guide". LinkedIn's policies say "Don't do things to artificially increase engagement with your content" ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/professional-community-policies)), and its spam page says it may limit content "designed to artificially increase engagement" ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1338787)). No LinkedIn page names keyword gating. MagicPost compared 684,000 posts with each author's own posts: keyword posts ran slightly above average until July 2026, then from the week of 27 July reached about half as many people, while the same creators' other posts fell about 7%. The cause is unknown and the study's figures disagree (52% against 62%). The alternatives are a genuine question, "DM me if you want it", the resource in Featured, or a warm-signal message offering it.

Published outreach benchmarks are a floor, because they describe cold, mostly automated campaigns: acceptance about 28.5% (Expandi, 13.2M attempts, [Expandi](https://expandi.io/blog/linkedin-outreach-benchmarks-2026/)) or 18.7% (Belkins 2026), replies to follow-ups about 10.4%, messages to existing connections 12.2%, and **1.3% of connected prospects booking a meeting** (Belkins). Warm, hand-written outreach should beat these. That is an inference; each expert should replace them with four weeks of their own numbers.

## Sales Navigator pays only once the daily routine exists

Core costs **$119.99 a month or $1,079.88 a year** on LinkedIn's current compare page ([LinkedIn](https://business.linkedin.com/sell/sales-navigator/compare-plans)), while an older LinkedIn page shows $99.99 ([LinkedIn](https://business.linkedin.com/sales-solutions/compare-plans)). Premium Business appears at $59.99 or $69.99 a month in third-party sources. InMail credits come from LinkedIn's plan pages and Help, per the newer note: Premium Career 5 a month, Premium Business 15, Premium All-in-One 30, Sales Navigator 50 and Recruiter Lite 30 ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a543695)). A credit returns if the recipient accepts, declines or replies within 90 days ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a541643)). One note reads a Sales Navigator rollover cap of 150 from Help; the newer note finds rollover caps only in third-party sources, so the cap is unconfirmed. Messages to first-degree connections are free and LinkedIn publishes no cap ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a554575)).

The notes' judgement, an inference, is that Sales Navigator is worth it once the expert posts consistently, has a defined client list, does the daily routine and has hit the free account's monthly search limit ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a564226)). At about $90 to $120 a month against a client worth thousands, it pays for itself with one extra client a year; the real cost is time. Before that, notification bells on key people, "Who viewed your profile" and a manual warm list do the job.

## The rules leave no room for automation at any volume

User Agreement section 8.2 governs. Its wording here comes from training knowledge, backed by LinkedIn's Help page that restates it and by 2026 secondary sources ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement)). It bans using "bots or other unauthorized automated methods to access the Services, add or download contacts, send or redirect messages, create, comment on, like, share, or re-share posts, or otherwise drive inauthentic engagement". It also bans software, including browser plugins, that scrapes the site, bypassing "caps on keyword searches or profile views", and copying information "without the consent of the content owner". The Help page is LinkedIn's word: "we don't permit the use of any third party software, including "crawlers", bots, browser plug-ins, or browser extensions that scrape, modify the appearance of, or automate activity on LinkedIn's website" ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1341387)). Automated activity "can result in temporary or permanent restriction of your account" ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1340522)). There is no carve-out for small volumes.

Pods are a named target. LinkedIn's 2026 newsroom post says: "We actively limit the reach of inauthentic activity, including engagement pods and automated comments", and that pods "are not allowed" ([LinkedIn](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/authentic-content-and-conversations)). Sachdeva, via press, said the goal is to make pods "entirely ineffective" ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-vows-to-take-action-against-engagement-pods-fake-engagement/804970/)). The "97% reach drop" for pods has no source.

Limits are mostly unpublished. LinkedIn says the weekly invitation restriction lasts a week; practice reports about 100 a week, up to 200 for strong accounts. Free notes are five a month at 200 characters on the old Help copy, three in 2026 reports, and 300 characters is reported for Premium. Event invitations are capped at 1,000 a week.

Publishing has one safe route. LinkedIn's own scheduler takes posts from 10 minutes to 3 months ahead ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1347212)). A member's own developer app can post through the self-serve "Share on LinkedIn" product with a Page attached, as developer blogs report. Per-post analytics through the API belong to the Community Management API, which LinkedIn says is "only available to registered legal organizations" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/linkedin/marketing/community-management/community-management-api-migration-guide?view=li-lms-2026-09)). Buffer and Hootsuite use official APIs and are the clearly compliant tools. Taplio and AuthoredUp run as browser extensions, a grey area; LinkedIn reportedly restricted Taplio in April 2025 ([Kondo](https://www.trykondo.com/blog/is-taplio-safe-understanding-the-risks-to-your-linkedin-account)). Kleo's extension was reportedly shut down in early 2026 and Shield closed on 18 May 2026 ([Postiv](https://postiv.ai/blog/shield-alternatives)). An AI agent clicking "Post", "Connect" or "Send" on linkedin.com is, on a plain reading of 8.2, an "unauthorized automated method". No 2026 LinkedIn statement on AI browser agents was found.

Prospect data has its own rules. Section 8.2 bans copying LinkedIn data without consent, so a pipeline must not be filled by scraping or enrichment tools. Under GDPR a solo expert is a controller, usually relying on legitimate interests, and Article 14 requires telling people who you are and why you hold their data at first contact. The right to object to direct marketing is absolute ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-object/)). The defensible store is the member's own Connections export plus notes they typed. That is the research team's reading, not legal advice.

## Count from the export and the log, because SSI is gone

Per-post analytics show impressions ("an estimate and may not be precise"), unique viewers, reactions, comments, reposts, saves and sends, and profile viewers and followers gained from each post ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a701208)). The creator analytics export runs through Me, View Profile, Analytics, Show all analytics, Post impressions, a 7 to 365 day range, then Export, and produces an .XLSX file ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a704175)). An earlier note gave the path from training knowledge; the newer note's Help path is used. Guides saying it needs Creator Mode or is a CSV are outdated. The sheets are DISCOVERY, ENGAGEMENT, TOP POSTS, FOLLOWERS and DEMOGRAPHICS, per one practitioner parser read in full ([GitHub](https://github.com/charlie947/social-media-skills)). TOP POSTS holds the top 50 as two side-by-side tables. **LinkedIn documents neither sheet names nor columns**, so a parser must find columns by name, and a real 2026 export should be recorded before relying on one.

The "Get a copy of your data" archive gives `Connections.csv` with First Name, Last Name, URL, Email Address, Company, Position and Connected On ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a566336); [GitHub](https://github.com/tdimino/claude-code-minoan)). An earlier note listed fewer columns; the newer list, consistent across three sources, is used. The file has "Notes:" lines above the header, may carry a byte-order mark, and leaves email mostly blank. Several numbers are on screen but not exported, so the member types them: weekly profile viewers ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a548046)), saves and sends, profile views and followers from each post, and any "AI slop" notice. The member's log supplies the rest: comments written, requests sent and accepted, messages and replies, conversations, calls, proposals and clients.

Do not use the Social Selling Index. The notes conflicted: one found reports it was still free in 2026, another that it was switched off around 7 to 8 September. The newer note settles it. LinkedIn's "From SSI to AI" page says "The SSI score no longer accurately reflects the modern sales environment" ([LinkedIn](https://business.linkedin.com/sell/resources/SSI)). LinkedScore saw a banner on 27 August 2026, lost access without Sales Navigator by 7 September, and saw the same on a Premium All-in-One account on 15 September ([LinkedScore](https://www.linkedscore.app/blog/linkedin-ssi-discontinued)). John Espirian wrote on 2 September that LinkedIn "has officially discontinued" it for free and Premium users ([Threads](https://www.threads.com/@johnespirian/post/DcyEQQajioQ/)). No formal notice exists. SSI is deprecated in practice, and it measured activity, not clients.

The weekly scorecard in the notes is a Build Room proposal, to recalibrate after four weeks.

| Weekly metric | Months 1 to 3 | Established |
|---|---|---|
| Posts | 3 | 3 |
| Substantive comments on others' posts | 25 to 50 | 25 to 50 |
| Profile viewers | Rising | Rising or steady |
| Connection requests (warm first) | 30 to 60 | 50 to 80 |
| Acceptance | 40%+ | 50%+ |
| Warm-signal DMs started | 10 to 15 | 15 to 25 |
| Reply rate on warm DMs | 25%+ | 30%+ |
| Real conversations | 3 to 5 | 8 to 12 |
| Calls booked | 1 to 2 | 3 to 5 |

Diagnosis reads down the funnel. Few profile views point to weak or off-pillar comments and posts. Views without accepts point to the profile. Accepts without replies point to the messages. Replies without calls point to a missing next step. Calls without clients point to the offer or the sales conversation.

## Expect calls in weeks and inbound in months

No large-sample study of time-to-first-client exists. Practitioner consensus is "months of consistency, not a viral week" ([Coachway](https://coachway.io/articles/how-to-get-coaching-clients-on-linkedin/)), with first paid clients around months 7 to 12 for consultants starting from zero, a blog projection ([Escape the Cubicle](https://escapethecubicle.substack.com/p/how-to-get-your-first-or-next-clients)). Welsh's first post reportedly got 12 views; a year later his posts got about 30,000 ([NineTwoThree](https://www.ninetwothree.co/blog/lessons-learned-from-business-builder-justin-welsh)). "Five clients in 30 days" courses rest on survivorship bias.

For an *established* expert with a reputation, an offer and a network, the notes infer two clocks, to be named at the start. **Warm outbound**, reactivating people who know the expert and messaging warm signals, can produce calls in weeks 1 to 4. **Content inbound** takes 3 to 6 months or more of consistent posting and commenting. Until then, judge content by leading numbers: profile viewers, followers from posts, saves, and comments from the right people.

## What the Build Room LinkedIn Engine does with this

The repository's skill (`/home/user/codex/buildroom/skills/buildroom-linkedin/SKILL.md`) already turns these findings into a system. Its thesis is that the feed matches by topic, conversations are the engine and posts the fuel, the member's experience is the moat, and the account is a business asset. Its references load in a fixed order, with account safety first and overriding even the member's request.

| Finding | Where the skill applies it |
|---|---|
| Topic-based ranking; 2 to 3 topics for 90 days | `references/content-system.md` §1: two or three pillars in the buyer's words, held 90 days; the post gate checks each draft names its pillar in the first two lines |
| Profile as landing page; button or Services | `references/profile-playbook.md`: Welsh's headline questions and four formulas, a one-promise banner, a seven-part About, one or two Featured items, the search-or-content rule for the button, Connect primary under about 5,000, a trust checklist |
| Generic AI text penalised; LinkedIn asks for review | `content-system.md` §2: a 20-minute voice-first capture and a story bank; nothing invented; every draft labelled "draft, edit before posting" with the facts it used |
| Documents and images lead; video for trust | `content-system.md` §7: text plus image by default, one document carousel a week, video handed to the Video Director, polls for research only |
| 2 to 5 posts a week; first 140 characters; 1,300 to 2,500 characters | Three posts a week in a fixed mix; writing rules and an AI-tell list; posts through the Kern framework, long stories through Halbert |
| Practitioner systems | A pillars × five-styles matrix after Welsh and 20 archetypes credited to Acosta, Barker, Welsh, Bloom, Hormozi, Donnelly and Alić; `references/source-credit.md` records the debts |
| Comments, notes, warm signals, DMs, follow-ups | `references/conversation-engine.md`: the daily 20 minutes, a three-tier watch list, comment templates K1 to K4, blank requests by default, an eight-signal table with "no signal, no DM", DMs D1 to D6 under about 400 characters, F1 and F2 then stop, voice note V1 as a warm second touch, Sales Navigator only when the routine exists |
| Section 8.2, pods, limits, keyword gating, GDPR | `references/account-safety.md`: nine "never" rules including no automation and no comment-gating, a member-by-hand list, a pacing guard (20 invitations a day or 80 a week, 5 free notes a month, 200 characters unless Premium, withdraw at three weeks), legitimate-interest records, `forget`, 12-month retention |
| Export-based measurement; SSI gone; two clocks | `references/pipeline-and-scorecard.md`: ten stages, a GoHighLevel "LinkedIn Organic" handoff, a weekly scorecard, starter targets labelled a proposal, the two clocks stated at the start |
| Claims tagged by strength | `references/linkedin-principles.md` tags every line [LinkedIn], [data], [practitioner] or [folklore] |

Three scripts carry the mechanics, and none touches LinkedIn. `scripts/li_pipeline.py` keeps a local log of people, signals and touches. It schedules F1 four days after a first message and F2 five days later, then stops, and flags invitations to check after 21 days. It prints the daily list, pacing warnings and retention flags, deletes a record on objection while keeping a suppression line, imports the member's `Connections.csv`, and writes tags and fields for the GoHighLevel push. `scripts/li_scorecard.py` reads the analytics `.xlsx` by column name rather than position, treats TOP POSTS as a sample, offers `inspect` to show what a new export holds, merges the numbers the member types, and writes a weekly scorecard with a four-week trend and a diagnosis. `scripts/render_linkedin.mjs` renders the banner with a profile-photo safe zone, post cards, Featured thumbnails and PDF document carousels from an approved spec, using real photos only, and checks overflow, text size and contrast.

The skill matches the evidence closely. A few lines should be tightened at its next refresh. `linkedin-principles.md` tags the August 2026 comment ranking as [LinkedIn], but only one trade source reports it, so it should read LinkedIn via press. The principles and content files weight Tuesday to Thursday, while Buffer's 2026 data rank Wednesday to Friday highest, so "midweek, test Friday" fits better. The pacing guard's five free notes rests on an old Help copy; the script already accepts `settings --notes-limit`, so a default of three, or asking, is safer until the live page is checked. And before trusting the scorecard parser, a member's real 2026 export should be run through `li_scorecard.py inspect` and recorded. The skill's core design is sound. The scarce inputs are the member's own: a clear topic, real stories, real proof and a daily hand-written conversation. The skill automates only what LinkedIn allows off the platform: drafting, queuing, counting and rendering. That keeps the account safe and the content specific, which are the two things the 2026 changes reward.
