━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
SECTION 1: THE CORE TRUTH ABOUT FOLLOW-UP
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Most businesses have follow-up. Almost none have a follow-up
SYSTEM. They have a welcome email, a "we miss you" blast, a
nurture sequence someone wrote in a hurry, and a CRM full of
people who raised their hand once and never heard anything that
moved them.

The truth:

PEOPLE DON'T BUY OFFERS. THEY BUY THE NEXT BELIEF THAT MAKES
THE OFFER FEEL INEVITABLE.

"The question we want to ask. This is the million dollar question.
Is, what do they need to believe to buy the next thing."
— 2026-04-22

That question is the whole engine. Every email, text, ad, and page
in a follow-up system exists to install ONE belief that the
prospect needs before the next step. Not ten. One.

"One email equals one belief shift. Never stack beliefs. Just like
every single step in a conversion process should only have one
decision. Same thing when you're trying to instill beliefs."
— 2026-05-27

The second truth, and the reason this is a machine and not a
writing exercise:

"Every business has a series of things that has to happen. A
series of yeses." — 2026-08-05

A prospect moves through a chain of yes/no decisions: opened,
clicked, watched, booked, showed, bought, stayed. Each decision
point is a place where people fall off. The Decision Machine puts
a follow-up campaign on every one of those points, so nobody
falls off in silence.

The third truth is about why owners never build this:

"This whole structure of follow-up and automation logic — if I gave
it to 100 people, the chances of all 100 nailing it are very slim.
The hard part is building all this automation. Building the
scaffolding is awful." — 2026-04-15

The emails are the easy part. The scaffolding — tags, conditions,
routing, removal, engagement tracking — is where everyone quits.
This week the scaffolding is generated. Your job is the beliefs
and the verification.

The fourth truth:

"If your offer sucks, none of it matters." — 2026-05-27

The Decision Machine amplifies. It cannot rescue a weak offer or a
vague avatar. That is why it sits after §2 and §3 in the Business
File, and why it reads them before it builds anything.

SECTION 2: THE THREE LAYERS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

"The decision machine is a few things. One of them, it's a
switching station, but more than being a switching station, it's
a psychology setup, too." — 2026-04-22

LAYER 1 — THE CONTROL LAYER (the switching station)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────

"The first layer is having a control system that manages all your
campaigns. Each set of boxes represents a campaign."

The control layer is one master automation that routes every
contact through your campaigns using a DECISION DIAMOND per
campaign:

"Once you start the decision machine, it goes into this decision
diamond. This is a decision point, and the decision is yes or no.
And the question is: have they seen this campaign? If they haven't,
we put them in the campaign. They go through it, and when they're
done, they come back to the start and go through the process again.
If they've already seen it, it bypasses the first campaign and goes
to the second: have I seen this yet? It just keeps on moving till
it finds a new campaign it hasn't seen."

In text:

   [Start / return point]
           |
      <Seen Campaign 1?> --no--> [Campaign 1] --done--> back to Start
           | yes
      <Seen Campaign 2?> --no--> [Campaign 2] --done--> back to Start
           | yes
      <Seen Campaign 3?> --no--> [Campaign 3] --done--> back to Start
           | yes
          ...  until it finds one they haven't seen

"Seen" is encoded as a tag: every campaign applies a completion tag
on exit, and the diamond checks for it. A campaign is entered by an
ENTRY CONDITION tag and left by an EXIT tag. Those two tags are
what you verify by hand on every campaign, every time.

LAYER 2 — THE BELIEF LAYER (what the campaign says)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────

"Instead of just having random nurture campaigns where you're just
trying to give them good stuff, if you're intentional about it,
your results will skyrocket. If you're gonna do it anyway, why not
do it right? It takes the same amount of time. You just add some
intelligence into it."

Each campaign carries the belief required for the NEXT step. The
belief map (Section 4) decides which belief goes where. Inside a
campaign, each message installs one belief shift, in order, all
serving one core belief for the sequence.

"I had one belief for the whole thing, and I was always driving
towards that core belief throughout the entire thing, which means
by the end of it, they're like, I want this thing."

A campaign is not email-only. "It could be text messages. I could
go into a custom audience on Facebook and Instagram within one of
these nurture campaigns and be pushing my content for that time
period that buyer is in. Emails, text messages, Facebook ads,
Instagram ads, LinkedIn. The same communication for the same thing
in that time-bound period."

LAYER 3 — THE AWARENESS LAYER (who the campaign is for)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The answer to "what do they need to believe?" changes with how
aware the prospect is. Eugene Schwartz's five levels, bottom up:

   1. UNAWARE — they know nothing. "The masses. Most expensive
      place to try and acquire customers."
   2. PROBLEM-AWARE — they know they have a problem, not that a
      solution exists.
   3. SOLUTION-AWARE — they know solutions exist, not that YOU
      exist.
   4. PRODUCT-AWARE — they know you, not what your solution is.
   5. MOST-AWARE — they know you and your solution; "they're at
      the deal stage, they just need to find the right offer."

"When you ask what do they need to be aware of to do the next
thing, the answer changes based on this level of awareness. And
what it requires is an education step. You start with some
education content, and the people that engage with that, you move
them up to the next stage."

The paid-traffic rule: "If you want to be successful at paid
advertising, you start at the low-awareness end. If you start at
level 5 with completely aware people, your chances of success in
advertising are almost zero."

The awareness layer assigns each campaign an ENTRY LEVEL. A
campaign written for level 2 reads as noise to level 5, and vice
versa.

SECTION 3: CONVERSION POINTS — WHERE CAMPAIGNS COME FROM
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

You do not invent campaigns. You find the yes/no decisions in your
funnel and put a campaign on each one.

THE UNDIRECTED FIRST PASS. "I asked: define every one of these
conversion points in the entire process. I didn't tell it what the
conversion points were, and I did that on purpose. A lot of times,
if I don't guide it, it's gonna come up with something I didn't
think of, and I want that. And then I give it the thing that I
want."

The result of one such pass, for a four-phase program funnel:
Compass Incomplete · Architect Path · Blueprint Ready · Build Brief
· Offer Unseen · Video Viewed · No Decision · Checkout Exception ·
Paid Incomplete · Strategy Invitation. Ten campaigns, 32 emails,
36 workflow actions, all with dynamic content.

"Did they view the video all the way through or not? Well, let's
create a video-view automation. I would have never taken the time
to do that."

For a service business, the conversion points usually look like:

   opted in but never opened · opened but never clicked · clicked
   but never booked · booked but never showed · showed but no
   decision · decided no · paid but never onboarded · onboarded
   but went quiet · finished but never referred · cold for 90+ days

Every one of those is a campaign with an entry tag, an exit tag,
one belief to install, and an awareness level.

SECTION 4: THE BELIEF MAP
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

THE FIVE BELIEFS. Before any campaign is written, the machine
states the five beliefs a prospect must hold to buy the core offer.
They are derived from the avatar's pains, objections, and
resistance patterns — in the Build Room, from §2 and §3 of the
Business File. A typical shape:

   Belief 1 — addresses the surface pain ("this is solvable")
   Belief 2 — addresses the identity pain ("I am not the problem")
   Belief 3 — reframes the problem ("the real cause was hidden")
   Belief 4 — establishes proof ("this person has done it")
   Belief 5 — creates anticipation ("the next step is obvious")

Every campaign in the inventory is then assigned the belief the
prospect needs at THAT conversion point, and every message inside
it is assigned one belief shift.

THE BELIEF-TO-STORY MODEL. Six belief stages plus a seventh,
closing email. "It determines which belief gets instilled in which
order. We're instilling beliefs when these emails are created."

   1. DISARM — belief: "my struggle is not because I'm broken,
      stupid, or lazy." Reader is defensive and guarded. Story
      lowers resistance and removes self-blame. ZERO promotion.
      "You're never, never, never, ever promoting in the disarm
      stage. Even mentioning an offer here damages trust."
   2. IDENTIFY — belief: "someone understands my exact situation."
      Reader is curious, leaning in. A mirror story with
      hyper-specific detail: "They finish reading and think,
      that's me." Still no promotion; foreshadowing at most.
      Identification runs on feelings, not situations: "if we
      talk about feelings, you can always relate to the feelings."
   3. REFRAME — belief: "the reason nothing worked before is
      because the real problem was hidden." Reader is hopeful,
      searching. The misdiagnosis story: everyone focuses on X,
      the real issue is Y. "You're introducing a concept, not a
      product."
   4. ILLUMINATE — belief: "there's a new way, and it actually
      makes sense." Reader is intellectually engaged. A discovery
      story: "I almost didn't believe this worked." Makes the new
      approach feel logical, not magical. Soft contextual mention
      only. This is seeding.
   5. AUTHORIZE — belief: "this person and this system are
      credible and safe to follow." Reader is trust-seeking,
      risk-aware. Origin story or case study as narrative.
      "Establish trust without bragging." Permission-based
      transition: "If you want to see how this works…" and "this
      may or may not be for you."
   6. INVITE — belief: "taking the next step is the obvious,
      low-risk choice." Reader is ready, looking for direction.
      Contrast action versus inaction. A clear call to action:
      invitation, not pressure. "This is where revenue arrives."
   7. SUNSET — the honest last resort, never early. "You honestly
      tell people they will be removed unless they act. Loss
      aversion activated. People want to assert control when
      something's being taken away. Honesty builds trust.
      Self-segmentation." Non-responders are removed.

Day numbers attached to stages in any template are placeholders.
"Don't read anything into that." Cadence comes from your list
(Section 7). For short runs (social, ads): "I almost grouped 1, 2,
and 3 together, 4 separate, 5 and 6 together. I wouldn't go 7
steps. 3 or 4 max to the offer."

THE STORYTELLING OVERLAY. "Each email is one episode, an unfolding
narrative. The reader is the audience, the product is a character
that emerges naturally through the story. Hyper-specific details
build trust. Two feelings after every email: that was worth
reading, and I need to know what happens next."

OPEN LOOPS (used, with one warning). "In a typical long-format
email, I'm opening 3 loops: one at the top that connects to the
end of the email, a second that goes somewhere in the future of the
sequence, and a third that's to the very next one." Track every
loop. "If you forget to close the loop, you break trust. At a
subconscious level."

THE STYLIST. Stages 1–5 are warm-up; the invite is conversion.
"Do the warm-up, and then Frank Kern." Halbert "if I need a click";
Kern for the long-form piece that sells something bigger. Off the
shelf "it's gonna sound like Frank. And you might not want to
sound like Frank." Train the voice on your own best emails.

SECTION 5: THE TAG ARCHITECTURE AND THE CONTROL LOGIC
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

The generated master document for one business carried a
56-tag architecture. The exact tags are generated per business;
the FAMILIES are constant:

   SOURCE TAGS — one per lead source, derived from where leads
      come from (§1, §6, §7). Applied at entry. Never removed.
   STATUS TAGS — where the contact is in the chain of yeses:
      lead · booked · showed · client · past client · referred.
   LEAD-AGE AND ROI TAGS — how long since entry, and what the
      contact has been worth. Drive reporting.
   ENGAGEMENT TAGS — last engagement bucket: under 30 days ·
      31–60 · 61–90 · over 90. Updated by the engagement tracker
      on opens, clicks, and replies.
   CAMPAIGN TAGS — per campaign: entry condition, in-progress,
      completed (the "seen" flag the diamond checks), exited-early.
   REMOVAL SCHEDULE — the rule for when temporary tags expire, so
      the machine does not accumulate stale state.

THE ENGAGEMENT TRACKER. An automation that re-buckets contacts by
last engagement. It feeds two things: the belief layer (a 90-day
silent contact is not in the same place as a yesterday-clicker)
and the broadcast stagger.

THE BROADCAST STAGGER (large lists only). For promotions, not
autoresponders. "Under 30 days I'm letting through. 31–60 go a day
later. 61–90, two days later. Over 90, five days later, because
everybody that matters has run through at this point." Plus a
random 1–10 segment on every new lead so sends go out in 10%
batches: "instead of sending 300,000 emails at once, I'm sending
30,000-person batches." The most engaged send positive signals
first, so later batches land in the inbox. "If you have 1,000 or
2,000, or even 5 or 10,000, this is overkill."

THE MASTER CONTROLLER. One workflow that runs the diamonds. Every
campaign returns to it. It never writes a message itself.

SECTION 6: BUILDING IT — WHAT THE MACHINE DOES AND WHAT YOU DO
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

The build is generated, then verified. Never the reverse.

WHAT GETS GENERATED: the conversion-point inventory, the belief
map, the tag architecture, the control logic, every campaign's
entry and exit tags, every message, the CRM build map, the
verification checklist, and the launch plan.

WHAT GETS BUILT BY AN AGENT (Claude Code, Codex, or Cowork with the
browser extension) IN YOUR CRM: tags, custom fields, workflows,
and messages. "I did not log in to GoHighLevel one time, except to
double-check the sequence."

WHAT YOU DO, WITHOUT EXCEPTION:
   - Confirm the five beliefs. They are yours, not the machine's.
   - Verify the entry tag and the exit tag on every campaign.
      "I wanted to check the condition tag that started the
      automation and the condition tag that took them out. It
      made a mistake on the takeout tag on 4 of them."
   - Read every message before it goes live.
   - Reply to humans yourself. "Don't break that with automated
      replies to genuine human responses. Having Claude create a
      draft reply is different. That's not an automated reply."

"I go by: don't trust, verify."

MECHANICAL VS CREATIVE. "Mechanical work doesn't need an advanced
thinking model, it just needs to run, so use a cheaper model. Build
me all the automations, no creative, no emails, do all that in the
cheap model, and then come back with the strong model and say, now
I need you to write killer emails, because these really matter."

ONE EMAIL AT A TIME. "Don't ever write 5 emails in the same output.
They'll never be good. Give me the first email. Now give me the
second email."

KNOWN LIMITS. Your CRM may not expose workflow creation through its
API; the agent then builds through the UI, which is slower and
mis-clicks. Expect it. Save progress as you go. The agent will
offer an off-ramp at the hard part; decide, don't drift.

SECTION 7: DELIVERABILITY, CADENCE, AND LIST HYGIENE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

A machine that lands in spam installs nothing.

   - Spam complaint rate under 0.3%, target under 0.1%. "If you
     write great emails, I promise you this isn't an issue."
   - SPF, DKIM, DMARC verified (mxtoolbox). One-click unsubscribe
     on every send.
   - Verify the list before the first send (a spam-trap filter
     such as ZeroBounce). Prune never-openers. Warm a cold list;
     "sending lists that are too cold too fast" is the second
     most common failure.
   - Replies are the strongest inbox signal. Invite them.
   - Cadence: "If your emails suck, you can't send too many of
     them. If your emails are amazing, you can send a lot of
     them." Don't be sleepy. The number is revenue per subscriber:
     research it for your niche and let it set frequency.
   - Write to one person. "Even with a list of 475,000 people, I
     was writing to one person."

SECTION 8: THE REACTIVATION VARIANT
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

"Somewhere in your CRM system is a list of people that raised
their hand but haven't done anything."

Reactivation is the Decision Machine pointed at a dormant list.
Why bother: 8–15% recovery is typical; reactivating is roughly ten
times cheaper than acquiring; a welcome-back email after
re-engagement sees far higher open rates; only about a quarter of
marketers run reactivation at all.

Why reactivations fail: the promotional blast · the single "we
miss you" · wrong tone (too salesy or too apologetic) · too cold
too fast · no cleanup afterwards · set-and-forget with nobody
answering replies.

How the variant differs:
   1. LIST AUDIT FIRST — verify, prune never-openers, warm up.
   2. STAGE-ORDERED, NOT CAMPAIGN-ORDERED — the seven stages run
      per person as one sequence, because a cold contact has to be
      walked back from Disarm.
   3. THE SUNSET EMAIL IS MANDATORY — honest removal notice,
      self-segmentation, last resort, never early.
   4. REPLIES ARE THE WIN — invite them; draft answers with AI;
      never auto-send.
   5. LAUNCH CADENCE — Day 1 list verification · Day 2 build the
      workflow · Day 3 smart lists · Day 4 warm sends.

A member with no dormant list still uses this shape for their
first-ever automated sequence: "it's not just reactivation."

SECTION 9: THE MOST COMMON FAILURES
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

1. RANDOM NURTURE. Good content with no belief assigned. Feels
   generous, converts nothing.
2. STACKED BELIEFS. Ten things they need to believe, crammed into
   one email. "No."
3. PROMOTING IN DISARM. The offer shows up in email one. Trust
   gone.
4. UNVERIFIED EXIT TAGS. The campaign never releases the contact,
   or releases them into nothing. Four of ten had this in a real
   build.
5. AUTOMATED HUMAN REPLIES. The machine answers a person.
6. BLAST RADIUS. "Automation multiplied reach. It also multiplied
   the blast radius of every error." Duplicate confirmations,
   double sends, a broken checkout at scale. Build recovery
   before scale; keep a send ledger; test the money path
   continuously.
7. SLEEPY CADENCE. Two weeks between emails because you're
   worried about "too many."
8. THE WHITEBOARD PHOTO. The belief map lived in one session and
   was never written down. It belongs in the Business File.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

When working through Step 2 prompts, Claude will apply these
principles to every output, automatically:

PRINCIPLE 1: ONE BELIEF PER MESSAGE, ONE CORE BELIEF PER SEQUENCE
Never stack. Never skip stages.

PRINCIPLE 2: CAMPAIGNS COME FROM CONVERSION POINTS
Undirected inventory first, then the member steers. Every
campaign has an entry tag, an exit tag, a belief, and an
awareness level.

PRINCIPLE 3: NO PROMOTION BEFORE AUTHORIZE
Disarm, Identify, Reframe, Illuminate carry no offer. Illuminate
may seed. Invite sells.

PRINCIPLE 4: ONE EMAIL AT A TIME
Every message is written in its own output, against its assigned
belief, and reviewed before the next.

PRINCIPLE 5: GENERATE THE SCAFFOLDING, VERIFY THE TAGS
The build map is complete and buildable by an agent. The member
verifies every entry and exit tag before anything goes live.

PRINCIPLE 6: HUMANS ANSWER HUMANS
The machine never auto-replies to a person. It may draft.

PRINCIPLE 7: DELIVERABILITY IS PART OF THE BUILD
Authentication, unsubscribe, list verification, and complaint
thresholds are checklist items, not advice.

PRINCIPLE 8: CHEAP FOR MECHANICAL, STRONG FOR CREATIVE
Model guidance is stated for every build step.

PRINCIPLE 9: THE FILE CARRIES THE MAP
The five beliefs, the campaign inventory with tags, and the build
status live in §16. The full pack lives in its own document.
