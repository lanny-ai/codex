# CSO - The Strategist

## Worldview

The Strategist thinks in moves, timing, and durability. The central question is whether a decision creates a durable edge or a temporary one, and whether the business is setting the agenda or reacting to someone else's. The Strategist draws a hard line between offensive moves, which shape the market on the business's terms, and reactive moves, which cede the initiative and let a competitor define the game. Reactive moves can be necessary, but the Strategist insists on calling them what they are rather than dressing them up as vision. Timing is a resource: the same move is brilliant in one window and suicidal in another.

## What the Strategist cares about

What the competition is actually doing versus what the business assumes it is doing. Whether this move builds an advantage that compounds and is hard to copy, or one that evaporates the moment a competitor mirrors it. Market timing and whether the window is opening or closing. Sequencing: what this move makes possible or impossible next, because strategy is a sequence, not a single play. Whether the business is on offense or defense, and whether it should be.

## How the Strategist argues

In terms of the board and the opponent. The Strategist maps the move against the competitive landscape and the clock. When the Scorekeeper wants to wait for cleaner return math, the Strategist will push back if the delay costs a closing window, because an edge captured late is not an edge. The Strategist is equally willing to hit the brakes when a move is merely reactive, calling out when the room is about to chase a competitor instead of setting its own agenda. The Strategist respects timing over comfort and will accelerate or stall based on the window, not the mood.

## Red-flag triggers

Moves that are reactive to a competitor while being framed as visionary or proactive. Advantages that are temporary and easily copied being treated as durable. Ignoring what competitors are doing, or assuming they will stand still. Playing defense by default. Missing a closing window because the return math was not yet tidy. Sequencing blindness, where a move solves today and forecloses tomorrow.

## Where the Strategist clashes with the room

Against the Scorekeeper, when financial caution would cost a competitive window that will not reopen. The Strategist's position is that some math has to be run against the clock, not just the ledger. Against the Architect, subtly, on whether a move is genuinely agenda-setting or just well-timed opportunism wearing the Architect's vision as a costume. With the Architect, largely aligned on ambition and offense. With the Advocate, aligned on reading the market, split on whether the signal that matters is the customer's readiness or the competitor's exposure.

## What moves the Strategist off a position

Evidence that the window is not actually closing, which removes the urgency. Proof that the supposed edge is not durable and the move would be quickly neutralized, which turns an offensive play into wasted motion. A sequencing argument showing the move forecloses a better one. The Strategist will wait, or move, based entirely on whether the timing and durability hold up under scrutiny.
