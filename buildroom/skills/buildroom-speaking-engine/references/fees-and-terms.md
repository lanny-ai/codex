# Fees and terms

Fees come second in this skill, and honesty about them comes first.

## What is known, and what isn't
- **There's no reliable fee data for a new speaker.** The averages quoted online describe established speakers on bureau lists. Fee tables on blogs are unsourced. Never quote a "typical" fee to a member.
- **Most of the stages a service business owner starts on are unpaid by policy:** service clubs, chambers, libraries, association breakouts. Associations often give free registration instead. Travel is often not covered. Ask.
- **Bureaus** take speakers who already have demand and are paid by commission. An upfront fee to "join a bureau" is a paid directory, not representation.
- Nobody can tell the member whether a fee they are offered is fair. They can only compare it with what the day costs them and what the room is worth.

## Deciding whether a stage is worth it
Three questions, in this order:
1. **Are my buyers in the room?** If yes, an unpaid stage can be worth more than a fee.
2. **What does it cost me?** A day, travel, a membership. Say the number out loud.
3. **What may I offer?** A stage that allows a free resource and conversations afterwards is worth more than one that allows nothing.

When the member's goal is a fee: a paid stage wants evidence the speaker is good. A few unpaid talks to the right rooms, a line from each host and a short clip are how that evidence is made.

## When a stage does pay
- Ask what their budget is before naming a figure.
- Quote one figure for the talk, and say what is included: preparation, the talk, questions, the handout. Decide beforehand the lowest figure at which the day is still worth doing, and keep it to yourself.
- **Trade, don't discount.** If the host asks for less, ask for something in return: a shorter talk, payment in advance, permission to mention the free resource, an introduction to two other hosts. A price cut with nothing in return teaches the host the first figure wasn't real.
- Travel is separate. Agree who books and who pays.
- "We have no budget" is usually true. Things a host can give instead: travel, a copy of the recording, the attendee list where attendees agreed to it, a line the member may quote, an introduction to two other hosts, a table to talk at afterwards, a paid workshop later. Ask for one or two, not all.
- For a real negotiation, **Sales Conversations** ("Help me with my sales call") has the method.

## What to have in writing
An email is enough for a local talk. For anything with money, travel or a recording, a short agreement. Either way it should say:
- the date, the time, the place, the length, the audience;
- the fee, or that there is none; when it is paid; a deposit if any;
- travel and lodging: who books, who pays;
- **what may be offered from the stage** and afterwards;
- **recording:** whether it is recorded, who may post it and where, and that the member keeps the rights to their talk and slides. If the agreement is silent on recording, ask;
- what happens if either side cancels;
- exclusivity, if the host asks the member not to speak nearby for a time;
- equipment: a microphone, a screen, a table.

This is a checklist, not legal advice. For a contract with real money or rights in it, the member has a lawyer read it. Terms that take all rights to the talk with nothing in return are a red flag (`vetting.md`).

## Claims from the stage, in plain words (not legal advice)
- A client story is a testimonial. It must be real and used with permission.
- If the result in a story isn't what clients generally get, the member says what clients generally get. "Results not typical" hasn't been a defence in the United States since 2023.
- No promise of income or results.
- If the member is paid to recommend something, or earns a commission (a tool, a summit), they say so.
- Images, video and music on slides need a licence or to be the member's own. "It was on the internet" isn't a licence.
