SECTION 1: THE CORE TRUTH ABOUT RUNNING A BUSINESS BY THE NUMBERS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

This month you documented your processes, defined a role, and
built the training. All of that is a machine. This week you build
the gauge on the machine.

The truth most owners learn too late:

YOU CANNOT MANAGE WHAT YOU ONLY FEEL.
A BUSINESS RUN ON HOW THE WEEK FELT IS A BUSINESS RUN ON MOOD.

"In God we trust. All others must bring data."
— attributed to W. Edwards Deming

Gino Wickman built the EOS Scorecard on one observation: owners
know their business is off two or three weeks after it went off,
because they find out from the bank balance or the angry client.
A handful of numbers, reviewed weekly, moves that discovery to the
week it happens.

The second truth, from Andy Grove, who ran Intel by this rule:

MEASURE THE INPUT, NOT JUST THE OUTPUT.

"A measurement — any measurement — should be an indicator of
output, not activity... Indicators tend to direct your attention
toward what they are monitoring."
— Andy Grove, High Output Management

Revenue is an output. It tells you what happened. By the time it
moves, the cause is a month old. The numbers that let you steer are
LEADING: proposals sent, discovery calls booked, reports delivered
on time, onboarding started. They move first. A dashboard of only
lagging numbers is a rearview mirror.

The third truth, and the one that makes the dashboard actually
change something:

A NUMBER WITHOUT A THRESHOLD IS TRIVIA.
A NUMBER WITHOUT AN OWNER IS A WISH.

Every metric on the page needs a line — above it, green; below it,
red — and one name next to it. Red numbers become issues. Issues
get decided in the weekly meeting. Without the threshold, the
meeting is a reading. Without the owner, the issue is nobody's.

The fourth truth, for the member who now has a hire:

THE DASHBOARD IS HOW YOU LET GO WITHOUT LOSING SIGHT.

Week 3 gave you the checkout — the moment you stop reviewing every
output. The dashboard is what replaces that review: not "did they
do it right?" every time, but "are the numbers that prove it green
this week?" That is the difference between delegating and abdicating.

SECTION 2: THE FOUR MASTER FRAMEWORKS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

FRAMEWORK 1 — THE EOS SCORECARD (Wickman, Traction)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The most widely adopted small-business dashboard model. Its rules:

   5-15 NUMBERS. Not 40. If everything is measured, nothing is
   watched. For a business of one to ten people: 5-9.

   WEEKLY. Monthly is too slow to steer; daily is noise for most
   service businesses. Weekly is the cadence at which a service
   business actually changes.

   ONE OWNER PER NUMBER. The person accountable for the number
   reports it — not the owner reading it off a system.

   A GOAL PER NUMBER. The threshold. Above = on track. Below = off.

   13 WEEKS VISIBLE. A rolling quarter of history so trends are
   visible, not just this week's snapshot. Three reds in a row is
   a different problem than one red.

   ACTIVITY-BASED, LEADING. Wickman's own rule: the scorecard
   should be mostly things people DO (calls made, reports sent),
   because those predict the results you want.

THE SCORECARD IS NOT THE MEETING'S AGENDA. It is the first five
minutes of it. Reds go to the issues list; the meeting solves
issues. Reviewing the scorecard for thirty minutes is the most
common way this tool dies.

FRAMEWORK 2 — LEADING AND LAGGING PAIRS (Grove; Kaplan & Norton)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

LAGGING indicators measure results: revenue, clients retained,
profit. They confirm. They cannot steer.

LEADING indicators measure the activities and intermediate outcomes
that produce results: proposals sent, calls booked, on-time delivery
rate, onboarding completed within 7 days. They predict. They steer.

GROVE'S PAIRING RULE: measure in pairs, so that pushing one number
does not quietly wreck another. Reports delivered on time — paired
with client corrections per report. Proposals sent — paired with
proposals accepted. Calls booked — paired with show-up rate. A
single unpaired metric gets gamed, usually by accident.

KAPLAN & NORTON'S BALANCE (the Balanced Scorecard, simplified for a
service business): don't let one perspective own the page. A useful
small-business dashboard touches four:
   MONEY       — what came in, what's owed, what's booked ahead
   CLIENTS     — pipeline, delivery, satisfaction signals
   OPERATIONS  — the SOPs: runs completed, on time, without exception
   PEOPLE      — the hire's ramp: checkouts passed, escalations, hours

FRAMEWORK 3 — THE CONSTRAINT (Goldratt, The Goal)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────

Theory of Constraints, in one sentence: every system has exactly one
bottleneck at a time, and improving anything other than the
bottleneck does not improve the system.

For a service business the constraint moves: this month it's lead
flow, next month it's delivery capacity, the month after it's the
owner's calendar. The dashboard's job is to make the current
constraint visible — usually as the number that is red most
consistently — and the weekly meeting's job is to spend its
problem-solving time THERE, not on whichever fire is loudest.

THE FIVE FOCUSING STEPS, adapted:
   1. Identify the constraint (the persistently red number)
   2. Exploit it (get everything possible out of it as-is)
   3. Subordinate everything else to it
   4. Elevate it (invest to expand it — a hire, a tool, a process)
   5. When it moves, go back to step 1

FRAMEWORK 4 — THE MEETING RHYTHM (Harnish, Scaling Up; Wickman's L10)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Verne Harnish's Rockefeller Habits and Wickman's Level 10 Meeting
converge on the same structure for a weekly operating meeting. For
a business of one to ten people, 30 minutes:

   0:00  SCORECARD (5 min) — every number, owner reads it, on/off
         track only. No discussion. Reds → issues list.
   0:05  ROCKS / PRIORITIES (3 min) — the 1-3 things that matter
         this quarter. On/off track.
   0:08  PEOPLE & PROCESS (5 min) — the hire's ramp (checkouts,
         escalations), SOP exceptions logged this week, anything
         the FAQ needed. Problems → issues list.
   0:13  ISSUES (15 min) — prioritize the list, solve the top 1-3,
         to a decision and an owner. Not all of them. The top ones.
   0:28  DECISIONS & TO-DOS (2 min) — restate what was decided,
         who owns it, by when. Log it.

THE RULES THAT KEEP IT ALIVE:
   - Same day, same time, every week. No cancellations; reschedule
     only for real emergencies.
   - Starts and ends on time.
   - Solo business: still hold it. Thirty minutes with the dashboard
     and the issues list, alone, is the weekly review. It works.
   - Decisions get written down. A meeting with no decision log did
     not happen.

SECTION 3: THE PSYCHOLOGY OF WHY DASHBOARDS DIE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

1. COLLECTING THE NUMBERS IS WORK, AND IT'S INVISIBLE WORK
   If updating the dashboard takes 40 minutes of digging through
   three tools, it stops after week three. Every number needs a
   source that takes under two minutes to read — or it gets
   automated, or it gets cut.

2. THE OWNER WATCHES REVENUE AND NOTHING ELSE
   Because revenue is the number that feels real. But it's the one
   number you can't act on this week. Leading indicators feel
   abstract until the first time they call a bad month three weeks
   early.

3. RED NUMBERS FEEL LIKE BLAME
   So people stop reporting honestly, or the threshold quietly
   drifts down. The fix is cultural and structural: a red number is
   information, not a verdict, and it goes to the issues list where
   the response is "what do we do?" not "whose fault?"

4. THE MEETING BECOMES A STATUS READ
   Everyone reports, nobody decides. Thirty minutes pass, nothing
   changes, and within a month the meeting is "optional." The
   issues block is the meeting. Protect it.

5. TOO MANY NUMBERS, TOO EARLY
   Fifteen metrics in week one, most of them with no history and no
   thresholds, and the page is noise. Start with five. Add one when
   a real question needs it.

SECTION 4: WHAT WORLD-CLASS OPERATORS DO DIFFERENTLY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

THEY DERIVE THE NUMBERS FROM THE SYSTEM THEY ALREADY BUILT
   Every SOP has a Definition of Done — that's a metric (runs
   completed to standard). Every scorecard outcome is a metric.
   Every checkout is a metric. Every funnel has three (opt-in,
   show-up, close). The dashboard is not invented; it is READ OFF
   the Business File.

THEY MAKE EVERY NUMBER CHEAP TO GET
   A saved view, a report the tool already produces, a tally the
   operator marks on the checklist. Two minutes per number or it
   doesn't make the cut.

THEY PAIR EVERY LEADING NUMBER WITH ITS QUALITY CHECK
   Speed with accuracy. Volume with conversion. Output with rework.

THEY KEEP THE PAGE TO ONE PAGE
   If it scrolls, it isn't a dashboard, it's a report.

THEY RUN THE MEETING EVEN WHEN IT'S JUST THEM
   The solo weekly review is where most of the leverage lives for
   a business of one.

THEY LET THE DASHBOARD ASSEMBLE ITSELF
   The best-run small businesses don't build the dashboard on
   Monday morning. It's waiting for them — assembled from the
   sources, reds flagged, the meeting agenda drafted. Whether that
   is a tool, an assistant, or a scheduled AI routine, the owner's
   Monday starts with the page, not with building the page.

THEY WRITE DOWN THE DECISION
   A one-line decision log — date, decision, owner, follow-up date —
   is the institutional memory of the business. It is also the raw
   material for the next SOP.

SECTION 5: THE MOST COMMON DASHBOARD FAILURES
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

FAILURE 1 — ALL LAGGING
   Revenue, profit, cash. True, and useless for steering.
   FIX: at least half the numbers are leading.

FAILURE 2 — NO THRESHOLDS
   Numbers with no green/red line. FIX: every metric gets a goal
   before it goes on the page.

FAILURE 3 — NO OWNER
   The owner reads every number. FIX: one name per number; the
   person who moves it reports it.

FAILURE 4 — EXPENSIVE TO UPDATE
   FIX: two-minute sources or automation; cut the rest.

FAILURE 5 — THE MEETING IS A READ-OUT
   FIX: five minutes on the scorecard, fifteen on issues.

FAILURE 6 — NO HISTORY
   Only this week's snapshot. FIX: 13 weeks visible.

FAILURE 7 — NO DECISION LOG
   FIX: every meeting ends with decisions written, owned, dated.

SECTION 6: ANATOMY OF THE ONE-PAGE DASHBOARD
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Six blocks, one page:

1. THE HEADER — week of, who's reporting, meeting date.
2. THE SCORECARD — 5-9 rows: metric · owner · goal · this week ·
   last 12 weeks · on/off track. Leading indicators first.
3. THE CONSTRAINT — one line: what the bottleneck is right now,
   and what's being done about it.
4. PEOPLE & PROCESS — the hire's ramp (checkouts passed / pending),
   SOP exceptions this week, FAQ additions.
5. ISSUES — the running list, prioritized, with the top 3 marked
   for this week's meeting.
6. DECISIONS — last week's decisions with owner and status; this
   week's, filled in during the meeting.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
SECTION 7: THE METRICS ALREADY IN YOUR BUSINESS FILE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

The dashboard is read off the file. Where each number comes from:

FROM §6 FUNNEL & AUTOMATION:
   opt-in rate · show-up rate · close rate · leads in / week ·
   follow-up sequence completions

FROM §9 OPERATIONS & SOPs:
   SOP runs completed / week · runs completed to Definition of Done ·
   exceptions logged · checklist compliance

FROM §10 TEAM & HIRING:
   the scorecard outcomes themselves (each is a metric with a
   timeframe) · funnel stage if hiring is in progress

FROM §11 TRAINING & ONBOARDING:
   checkouts passed / pending · escalations this week ·
   questions captured to FAQ

FROM §3 SIGNATURE OFFER / §1 SNAPSHOT:
   proposals sent · proposals accepted · revenue booked ·
   active clients · hours the owner spent IN the business

THE OWNER-HOURS METRIC deserves its own line. Track the hours the
owner personally spent running documented processes. It should
fall every week the hire ramps. If it doesn't, the delegation
isn't happening — and that is the single most important red on
the page for a member who just made their first hire.

SECTION 8: THE AUTOMATED MONDAY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

A dashboard that must be assembled by hand every week will be
assembled by hand for about six weeks. The way to make it permanent
is to make its assembly someone else's job — or a scheduled routine's.

WHAT A SCHEDULED ROUTINE CAN DO EVERY MONDAY:
   - Read the Business File (the metric list, owners, thresholds)
   - Read the sources the member has connected or listed (a
     spreadsheet, a CRM export, the checklist tallies)
   - Assemble the one-page dashboard with reds flagged
   - Draft the 30-minute meeting agenda from the reds and the
     open issues
   - Deliver it — to the member's inbox, a shared doc, or the
     Business File itself — before the meeting

WHAT IT SHOULD NOT DO WITHOUT ASKING:
   - Change a threshold
   - Close an issue
   - Write a decision
   Those are the member's. The routine prepares; the member decides.

THE OPT-IN RULE: the routine is offered, never imposed. The member
sees exactly what it reads, what it produces, where it sends it,
and how to stop it — before it is created. A member who prefers to
assemble the page by hand for the first month, to learn the numbers,
is making a reasonable choice.

THE HONESTY RULE: if a number can't be read automatically (it lives
in the member's head, or in a tool that isn't connected), the
routine says so and leaves a blank for the member to fill — it
never estimates.

SECTION 9: THE OPERATING PRINCIPLES CLAUDE WILL APPLY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

When working through Step 2 prompts, Claude will apply these
principles to every output, automatically:

PRINCIPLE 1: READ THE METRICS OFF THE FILE
The dashboard is derived from the Business File — funnel metrics,
SOP runs, scorecard outcomes, checkouts, owner hours — never
invented from a generic template.

PRINCIPLE 2: FIVE TO NINE, LEADING FIRST
Never more than nine numbers. At least half are leading indicators.

PRINCIPLE 3: EVERY NUMBER HAS A GOAL, AN OWNER, AND A SOURCE
No metric goes on the page without a threshold, one name, and a
source that takes under two minutes to read.

PRINCIPLE 4: PAIR SPEED WITH QUALITY
Every leading indicator is paired with the number that catches it
being gamed.

PRINCIPLE 5: ONE PAGE, THIRTEEN WEEKS
The dashboard fits on one page and shows a rolling quarter.

PRINCIPLE 6: THE MEETING SOLVES ISSUES
Five minutes on the scorecard, fifteen on the top issues, every
meeting ends in written decisions with owners and dates.

PRINCIPLE 7: NAME THE CONSTRAINT
Every dashboard states the current bottleneck in one line, and the
meeting's problem-solving time goes there first.

PRINCIPLE 8: OFFER THE ROUTINE, NEVER IMPOSE IT
The automated Monday is presented with full transparency — what it
reads, produces, sends, and how to stop it — and created only on
the member's explicit yes. It prepares; it never decides.

PRINCIPLE 9: NEVER ESTIMATE A NUMBER
A metric that can't be read from a source is left blank and
flagged, not guessed.
