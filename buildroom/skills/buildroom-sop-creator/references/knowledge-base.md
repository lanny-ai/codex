SECTION 1: THE CORE TRUTH ABOUT SOPs
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

The single insight that separates a business from a job that owns you:

YOU DON'T HAVE A BUSINESS. YOU HAVE A COLLECTION OF THINGS
ONLY YOU KNOW HOW TO DO.

Michael Gerber spent forty years watching small businesses fail and
found the same cause almost every time. The owner was a technician
who got good at the work, went out on their own, and accidentally
bought themselves the worst job they ever had.

His prescription was one sentence:

"Work ON your business, not IN it."
— Michael E. Gerber, The E-Myth Revisited

And the mechanism for doing that is the Franchise Prototype: build
the business as though you intend to sell 5,000 clones of it, run
by ordinary people, without you present. You will probably never
franchise. That's not the point. The point is that designing for
replication forces every fuzzy, in-your-head process into daylight.

The second foundational truth, from the factory floor:

"Where there is no standard, there can be no kaizen (improvement)."
— Taiichi Ohno, architect of the Toyota Production System

This is the one most service businesses miss. They think SOPs are
about control, or about training a hire they haven't made yet.
They are not. A documented process is the ONLY thing you can
measure, test, and improve. Without a standard, every attempt at
improvement is just a different kind of guessing.

The third truth, and the one that changes behavior:

A PROCESS THAT LIVES IN YOUR HEAD CHARGES YOU RENT EVERY SINGLE DAY.

Not once. Every day. Every time you re-decide something you already
decided, re-explain something you already explained, or personally
catch an error a checklist would have caught, you pay again for work
you already did.

The member's job today is not "write SOPs." It is: stop paying rent
on the three or four processes charging the most.

SECTION 2: THE FOUR MASTER FRAMEWORKS

FRAMEWORK 1 — THE FRANCHISE PROTOTYPE (Gerber)
───────────────────────────────────────────────

Gerber's rules for building the prototype, adapted for a service
business of one to ten people:

RULE 1: The system runs the business. The people run the system.
   If a good outcome depends on a specific person's judgment,
   that is not a system. That is a dependency.

RULE 2: It must be operable by someone with the lowest possible
   level of skill — consistent with the outcome.
   "Lowest possible skill" does not mean unskilled. It means: what
   is the least specialized person who can run this and still hit
   the standard? Design for them, not for you.

RULE 3: Every piece of work is documented in an Operations Manual.
   Not a binder nobody opens. A living reference the work is
   actually performed from.

RULE 4: The business delivers a uniform, predictable result.
   Predictability is the product. Clients pay premium prices for
   the absence of surprises.

THE THREE ROLES TEST
Gerber's most useful diagnostic. Every owner wears three hats:
   TECHNICIAN  — does the work
   MANAGER     — plans, orders, supervises the work
   ENTREPRENEUR — designs the business that does the work

Most owners live at 90% Technician. Every SOP written moves an hour
from Technician to Entrepreneur, permanently. That is the actual
return on this session — not "documentation," but reallocated hours.

FRAMEWORK 2 — CHECKLIST ARCHITECTURE (Gawande)
───────────────────────────────────────────────

Atul Gawande's The Checklist Manifesto is the most rigorously
evidenced work on procedural documentation in existence, because
its failures killed people.

THE KEY DISTINCTION — two kinds of checklist:

   DO-CONFIRM: The person does the work from memory and expertise,
   then PAUSES at a defined point and confirms everything was done.
   Use for: experienced people doing familiar work. Preserves flow
   and judgment. Most of your SOPs should be this.

   READ-DO: The person reads each item and performs it, like a
   recipe. Use for: rare procedures, emergencies, high-consequence
   sequences, and genuinely new people.

Choosing wrong is why SOPs get abandoned. Handing an experienced
operator a READ-DO checklist for daily work insults them and slows
them down, so they stop using it. Handing a new hire a DO-CONFIRM
checklist for something they've never done gives them nothing.

GAWANDE'S DESIGN CONSTRAINTS (these are load-bearing):
   - Five to nine items. Beyond that, compliance collapses.
   - Fits on one page. If it scrolls, it won't be used.
   - Defines a PAUSE POINT — the specific moment work stops and the
     checklist is run. A checklist without a trigger moment is a
     document, not a checklist.
   - Contains only the KILLER ITEMS: the steps that are (a) most
     often missed and (b) most damaging when missed. Not every step.
   - Uses the exact language of the people doing the work.

THE EVIDENCE: The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist — nineteen items,
one page — was tested across eight hospitals worldwide and
published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2009. Major
complications and deaths both fell substantially. Peter Pronovost's
five-item central-line checklist in Michigan ICUs (the Keystone
project) drove infection rates toward zero and held for months.

Neither introduced new knowledge. Every clinician already knew
every item. The checklist only ensured that what was known got
done, under pressure, every time. That is the entire mechanism —
and it is exactly what a service business needs.

FRAMEWORK 3 — STANDARDIZED WORK (Toyota Production System)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Toyota's standardized work has three elements. Translated out of
manufacturing:

   1. THE SEQUENCE — the exact order of steps.
      Order is not cosmetic. Wrong order creates rework.

   2. THE TIME — how long the process should take.
      This is the element service businesses always skip, and
      skipping it is why they can't price or staff accurately.
      A process without a time estimate cannot be scheduled,
      quoted, or delegated with confidence.

   3. THE STANDARD — what "done correctly" looks like, concretely
      enough that two different people would agree.

THE KAIZEN LOOP: standard → run it → find the friction →
improve the standard → re-document. The SOP is not the finish
line. It is the baseline that makes improvement measurable.

THE JIDOKA PRINCIPLE (build quality in, don't inspect it in):
Catch the error at the step where it happens, not at final review.
Every quality gate you push earlier in the process is worth more
than any amount of checking at the end.

FRAMEWORK 4 — SIPOC + RACI (process boundaries and ownership)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Two tools that answer the questions that kill most SOPs.

SIPOC defines where a process starts and stops:
   SUPPLIERS  — who or what feeds this process
   INPUTS     — what must exist before it can start (the trigger)
   PROCESS    — the steps
   OUTPUTS    — what exists when it's done
   CUSTOMERS  — who receives the output and what they expect

Most broken SOPs are broken at the boundaries, not in the middle.
The steps are fine; nobody agreed on what starts it or what "done"
produces. Define the trigger and the output first, then the middle
almost writes itself.

RACI assigns ownership per step:
   RESPONSIBLE — does the work
   ACCOUNTABLE — owns the outcome (exactly ONE person, always)
   CONSULTED   — gives input before
   INFORMED    — told after

The single most common ownership failure: two people Accountable,
which means nobody is. If a step has two names on it, the process
will fail there.

SECTION 3: THE PSYCHOLOGY OF WHY SOPs DON'T GET USED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Most SOP projects fail. Not because the documents are wrong —
because of predictable human behavior. Design against all five.

1. THE CURSE OF KNOWLEDGE
   Once you know something, you cannot remember not knowing it.
   Experts skip steps that feel obvious and are not. The fix is
   never "try harder to be thorough." It is: have someone who
   doesn't know the process attempt it from the document, and
   watch where they stop.

2. DOCUMENTATION FEELS LIKE UNPAID WORK
   Writing an SOP produces no visible output today. The payoff is
   deferred and invisible. This is why SOP projects start on
   Monday and die on Wednesday. The fix is to document DURING the
   work — capture the process the next time you actually run it,
   not in a special writing session.

3. THE PERFECTION TRAP
   Owners try to document everything, get overwhelmed by the scope,
   and document nothing. Four excellent SOPs beat forty mediocre
   ones, and beat zero by an infinite margin.

4. THE ABANDONED-ARTIFACT PROBLEM
   An SOP that isn't where the work happens does not exist. If the
   process runs in a CRM and the SOP lives in a Google Doc nobody
   opens, the SOP has failed regardless of quality. Put the checklist
   where the hands are.

5. STALENESS KILLS TRUST FASTEST
   One wrong step teaches the reader the whole document is
   unreliable, and they stop consulting all of them. A living SOP
   library needs an owner and a review date, or it decays into
   fiction within a quarter.

SECTION 4: WHAT WORLD-CLASS OPERATORS DO DIFFERENTLY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

THEY DOCUMENT WHILE DOING, NOT AFTER
   The next time the process runs, they record it — screen capture,
   voice memo, or notes taken live — and turn the recording into the
   document. Recall-based documentation always omits the steps that
   have become automatic, which are frequently the steps that break.

THEY WRITE FOR THE PERSON WHO REPLACES THEM
   Not for themselves, not for a hypothetical star hire. For a
   competent person on their first week who has never seen this
   business. That reader forces the right level of detail.

THEY START WITH THE PROCESS THAT HURTS
   Not the easiest to document. The one that most recently caused
   an error, a late delivery, an awkward client email, or a Sunday
   night of catching up.

THEY SEPARATE REFERENCE FROM CHECKLIST
   Two artifacts, two jobs. The SOP is the full reference — read
   once when learning, consulted when something is unusual. The
   checklist is the one-page working tool used every single run.
   Collapsing them into one document produces something too long
   to use and too thin to learn from.

THEY NAME AN OWNER AND A REVIEW DATE ON EVERY DOCUMENT
   Unowned documents rot. A name and a date on the header is the
   cheapest possible defense.

THEY DEFINE "DONE" IN OBSERVABLE TERMS
   Not "make sure the report looks professional."
   Instead: "every chart has a titled axis, the client's name is
   spelled correctly in the header, and the file is named
   [Client]_[Month]_Report.pdf."
   If two people could disagree about whether a step is complete,
   the step is not yet written.

THEY BUILD THE ESCALATION PATH INTO THE DOCUMENT
   Every SOP names what to do when reality doesn't match the
   document: who to ask, how fast, and what the person is
   authorized to decide alone. Without this, delegation stalls
   at the first exception — and there is always an exception.

SECTION 5: THE MOST COMMON SOP FAILURES
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

FAILURE 1 — THE NOVEL
   Fourteen pages of prose nobody reads. Symptom of writing to
   prove thoroughness rather than to enable action.
   FIX: Steps as numbered imperatives. One action per step. Push
   background and rationale into a short "Context" block at the top
   or a "Notes" block at the bottom, never into the step list.

FAILURE 2 — THE VAGUE VERB
   "Review the deliverable." "Handle the intake." "Follow up
   appropriately." These read like steps and instruct nothing.
   FIX: Every step starts with a concrete verb and names the
   object, the tool, and the finish condition.

FAILURE 3 — NO TRIGGER
   The SOP describes what to do but never says when it starts.
   The process then runs only when someone remembers it.
   FIX: Line one of every SOP is the trigger — the event, time, or
   signal that starts it.

FAILURE 4 — WRITTEN FOR THE AUTHOR
   Full of internal shorthand, unnamed tools, and assumed access.
   FIX: Name every tool by its real name. State where access comes
   from. Expand every abbreviation on first use.

FAILURE 5 — NO OWNER, NO DATE
   Nobody knows whether it's current, so nobody trusts it.
   FIX: Owner and last-reviewed date in the header of every document.

FAILURE 6 — DOCUMENTING THE WRONG PROCESSES FIRST
   Owners document what's easy or what they enjoy, and leave the
   painful bottleneck undocumented because it's messy — which is
   exactly why it needs documenting.
   FIX: Triage by pain and frequency before writing anything.

FAILURE 7 — NO EXCEPTION PATH
   The document covers the happy path only. The first unusual case
   sends the person back to the owner, and delegation collapses.
   FIX: An "If this doesn't apply" branch on high-variance steps,
   plus a standing escalation rule.

SECTION 6: THE ANATOMY OF AN SOP THAT ACTUALLY GETS USED
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Nine components. Every one earns its place.

1. TITLE — verb + object. "Onboard a New Client," not "Onboarding."
2. HEADER BLOCK — owner, last reviewed, review cadence, version.
3. PURPOSE — one sentence: why this exists and what breaks without it.
4. TRIGGER — the exact event that starts this process.
5. INPUTS & ACCESS — what must exist and what logins are needed
   before step one. Missing access is the single most common
   stall point for a delegated process.
6. THE STEPS — numbered, one action each, concrete verb first,
   with the responsible role and a time estimate per step or phase.
7. DEFINITION OF DONE — observable completion criteria. What exists
   now that didn't before, and how would an outsider verify it?
8. EXCEPTIONS & ESCALATION — the common non-standard cases, what
   the operator may decide alone, and who to ask otherwise.
9. LINKED ASSETS — templates, files, saved views, snippets, and the
   one-page checklist derived from this SOP.

THE TWO-ARTIFACT RULE
Every documented process produces exactly two things:
   THE SOP        — the reference. Read to learn. Consulted for exceptions.
   THE CHECKLIST  — 5-9 killer items, one page, used on every run.
The checklist is derived FROM the SOP, never instead of it.

SECTION 7: WHICH PROCESSES TO DOCUMENT FIRST
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

You cannot document everything, and attempting it is the most
reliable way to document nothing. Triage on four dimensions.

SCORE EACH PROCESS 1-5:

   FREQUENCY   — how often does it run?
                 (Daily = 5. Once a quarter = 1.)
   PAIN        — how much does it cost when done wrong?
                 (Lost client = 5. Mild annoyance = 1.)
   OWNER-LOCK  — how completely does it depend on you personally?
                 (Only you can do it = 5. Anyone can = 1.)
   VARIANCE    — how differently does it go each time it runs?
                 (Different every time = 5. Identical = 1.)

PRIORITY SCORE = FREQUENCY × PAIN × OWNER-LOCK

Variance is scored but NOT multiplied in — it selects the format:
   Low variance  (1-2) → checklist-led. The steps are stable.
   High variance (4-5) → decision-tree-led. Document the decision
                         rules and branches, not a rigid sequence.

THE PARETO RULE FOR SOPs
In most service businesses, three to five processes generate the
large majority of recurring errors and owner interruptions. Those
are the month's work. Everything else waits.

THE FOUR USUAL SUSPECTS in service businesses — start here unless
the member's own triage says otherwise:
   1. Client onboarding (highest pain, highest first impression)
   2. The core delivery cycle (highest frequency)
   3. Reporting / client communication (highest owner-lock)
   4. Intake-to-proposal (highest revenue consequence)

SECTION 8: REAL-WORLD PATTERNS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

THE AVIATION PATTERN — checklists over expertise
Commercial aviation is the most-studied high-reliability system in
existence, and it runs on checklists used by people with thousands
of hours of experience. Expertise is not the alternative to a
checklist; the checklist is what lets expertise be spent on the
non-routine. Apply this directly: your most experienced person
needs a checklist most, because they are the most likely to
operate on autopilot.

THE SURGICAL PATTERN — the pause point
The WHO checklist works partly because of when it runs: defined
pauses before anesthesia, before incision, before leaving the room.
The lesson for a service business is that a checklist needs a
scheduled moment, not just a location. "Before the deliverable
goes to the client" is a pause point. "Somewhere during the
project" is not.

THE FRANCHISE PATTERN — documentation as an asset
A franchise sells its operations manual. That is literally the
product. The same logic applies to a service business at exit:
a business whose processes live in the owner's head is a job for
sale, and priced like one. A business with a working operations
manual is an asset. Members should understand that this month's
work compounds into enterprise value, not just convenience.

THE MICHIGAN ICU PATTERN — simple beats sophisticated
Pronovost's checklist had five items, all of them things every
clinician already knew. The lesson is not to build a comprehensive
system. It is to identify the handful of steps that are known,
skippable, and consequential — and make skipping them impossible.

SECTION 9: THE OPERATING PRINCIPLES CLAUDE WILL APPLY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

When working through Step 2 prompts, Claude will apply these
principles to every output, automatically:

PRINCIPLE 1: TRIAGE BEFORE DOCUMENTING
Never start writing SOPs until the processes are scored and ranked.
Documenting the wrong process well is still wasted effort.

PRINCIPLE 2: ONE ACTION PER STEP, CONCRETE VERB FIRST
Every step begins with an imperative verb and names the object,
the tool, and the finish condition. No vague verbs survive.

PRINCIPLE 3: OBSERVABLE DONE
Every step and every SOP defines completion in terms two different
people would agree on. If it can be argued about, it gets rewritten.

PRINCIPLE 4: WRITE FOR THE REPLACEMENT
The reader is a competent person in their first week who has never
seen this business. Not the owner. Not a star hire.

PRINCIPLE 5: TWO ARTIFACTS, ALWAYS
Every documented process yields an SOP (the reference) AND a
5-9 item one-page checklist (the working tool). Never one without
the other.

PRINCIPLE 6: EVERY PROCESS HAS A TRIGGER AND AN OWNER
No SOP is complete without the event that starts it and the single
accountable person. Two accountable names means none.

PRINCIPLE 7: BUILD THE EXCEPTION PATH
Every SOP states what happens when reality doesn't match it: what
the operator may decide alone, who to escalate to, and how fast.
Delegation fails at the first unhandled exception.

PRINCIPLE 8: TIME EVERY PROCESS
Every SOP carries a realistic time estimate. Untimed processes
cannot be scheduled, quoted, staffed, or delegated with confidence.
