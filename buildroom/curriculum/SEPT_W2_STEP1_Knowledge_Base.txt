SECTION 1: THE CORE TRUTH ABOUT HIRING
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

The single most expensive mistake a small service business makes:

YOU CANNOT HIRE WELL FOR A ROLE YOU HAVEN'T DEFINED.
AND YOU CANNOT DEFINE A ROLE YOU HAVEN'T DOCUMENTED.

Most owners hire the way they'd buy a used car from a stranger at
night: a vague sense of what they want, a conversation that goes
well, a gut feeling, and a signature. Then they spend three months
discovering that "good with clients" and "organized" meant different
things to each of them.

Geoff Smart and Randy Street studied thousands of hires for their
book Who and found the same thing the research had been saying for
decades:

"The number one problem in hiring is not a lack of good candidates.
It is a lack of clarity about what the job actually is."
— Geoff Smart & Randy Street, Who: The A Method for Hiring

Last week you documented the processes that were charging you rent.
That documentation is not a byproduct of hiring. It IS the role
definition. The SOPs you wrote are the job. The checklist is the
first-week training. The Definition of Done is the performance
standard. The exception path is the authority level.

This week you turn those documents into three things:
   1. A scorecard that defines success in the role in outcomes
   2. An ad that attracts the right person and repels the wrong one
   3. A structured interview that predicts performance instead of
      measuring charm

The second foundational truth, from the research:

UNSTRUCTURED INTERVIEWS ARE BARELY BETTER THAN A COIN FLIP.

The best-known meta-analysis in the field — Frank Schmidt and John
Hunter's review of 85 years of selection research — found that
unstructured interviews are among the weaker predictors of job
performance, while structured interviews and work samples are among
the strongest. Two people talking freely for forty-five minutes
mostly measures how much they like each other.

The third truth, and the one owners resist most:

THE FIRST HIRE IS NOT "SOMEONE LIKE ME."
IT IS "SOMEONE WHO RUNS THE SYSTEM I BUILT."

Gerber's rule again: the system runs the business, the people run
the system. You are not looking for a mini-founder. You are looking
for a reliable operator of documented processes, with the judgment
to escalate when reality doesn't match the document. That is a
different person, hired a different way, and — good news — a far
larger pool.

SECTION 2: THE FOUR MASTER FRAMEWORKS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

FRAMEWORK 1 — THE SCORECARD (Smart & Street, "Who")
────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The scorecard replaces the job description. A job description lists
duties. A scorecard defines what success looks like. Three parts:

   MISSION — one sentence. Why this role exists. What it is for.
      "Run client delivery so that every client gets their
      deliverables on time without the owner touching the work."

   OUTCOMES — three to five specific, measurable results the person
      must deliver in the first 6-12 months. Not activities. Results.
      Bad:  "Manage client reporting."
      Good: "Every monthly client report delivered by the 3rd
             business day, with zero owner corrections, by month 3."

   COMPETENCIES — the behaviors and traits required to hit those
      outcomes in THIS business. Usually 5-8. Include the cultural
      ones — the things that made your worst hire unbearable.

WHY IT WORKS: An outcome-based scorecard lets you evaluate every
candidate against the same standard, write an ad that attracts
people who can hit those outcomes, and — critically — evaluate the
hire honestly at 90 days. You cannot measure "manages reporting."
You can measure "zero corrections by month 3."

THE A METHOD SEQUENCE:
   SCORECARD → SOURCE → SELECT → SELL
Most owners jump to SOURCE (post an ad) with no scorecard, then
SELECT by feel. The ad quality and the interview quality are both
downstream of the scorecard. Fix the top of the sequence.

FRAMEWORK 2 — PERFORMANCE-BASED HIRING (Lou Adler)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────

Adler's insight: define the job by what the person must DO in the
first year, not what they must HAVE on day one.

   THE PERFORMANCE PROFILE — 5-6 "performance objectives," each a
   specific deliverable with a timeframe. This is close to the
   scorecard's outcomes, with an emphasis on the FIRST 90 DAYS,
   which is where new hires succeed or fail.

   THE MOST IMPORTANT QUESTION — Adler's one-question interview:
   "What is the single most significant accomplishment in your
   career, and walk me through it?" Then spend 15-20 minutes
   drilling into it: the situation, the results, the obstacles,
   what they did personally versus the team, what they learned,
   what they would do differently. The depth of the answer
   predicts performance far better than a list of skills.

   SKILLS vs ACCOMPLISHMENTS: A skill list tells you what someone
   claims to know. An accomplishment tells you what they actually
   did with it under real conditions. Always trade the first for
   the second.

WHY IT MATTERS FOR SMALL BUSINESSES: The best candidate for
"operator of my documented processes" often lacks the credential
that a duties-based description would demand — and has exactly
the track record of reliably finishing things that the role
actually needs. Performance-based hiring finds them.

FRAMEWORK 3 — STRUCTURED INTERVIEWING (the evidence base)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Structure means: every candidate gets the same questions, in the
same order, scored against the same written rubric, by evaluators
who score independently BEFORE they discuss.

THE FOUR ELEMENTS OF STRUCTURE:
   1. PREDETERMINED QUESTIONS tied to the scorecard competencies
   2. BEHAVIORAL FORMAT — "Tell me about a time when..." — because
      past behavior in similar situations is the best available
      predictor of future behavior. Use STAR to probe:
      Situation, Task, Action, Result — and always push to "what
      did YOU do," not "what did the team do."
   3. A SCORING RUBRIC written BEFORE the interview: what does a
      1, 3, and 5 look like for each competency, in observable terms
   4. INDEPENDENT SCORING before any discussion — Daniel Kahneman's
      finding from designing the Israeli army's officer selection:
      rate each attribute separately, on a defined scale, and only
      form an overall judgment at the end. Otherwise the first
      strong impression contaminates every subsequent rating.
      (Thinking, Fast and Slow, ch. 21)

THE WORK SAMPLE — the most predictive tool you have. Have the
candidate actually do a slice of the job, ideally a real task from
one of your SOPs, under realistic conditions, paid if it takes more
than an hour. A person who can run your client-report SOP from the
document, with the checklist, in the time estimated, has just
proven more than any interview could. Google's re:Work guidance
puts work samples and structured interviews at the top of the
predictive list for the same reason.

WHAT STRUCTURE PROTECTS YOU FROM:
   - Halo effect (one strong trait coloring everything)
   - Similarity bias (hiring people who remind you of you)
   - Confidence bias (mistaking articulate for competent)
   - The "great conversation" trap
   - Legal exposure from inconsistent, undocumented decisions

FRAMEWORK 4 — TOPGRADING'S CHRONOLOGICAL WALK (Brad Smart)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The core tool from Topgrading, adapted for a small business:

For each of the candidate's last 2-3 roles, in order, ask:
   1. What were you hired to do?
   2. What accomplishments are you most proud of?
   3. What were the low points? What went wrong?
   4. Who was your manager, and — when we call them — what will
      they say your strengths and weaker areas were?
   5. Why did you leave?

The fourth question is the engine. Telling a candidate you WILL
call their former manager, and asking them to predict what that
manager will say, produces dramatically more honest answers about
weaknesses. It is not a trick — it is a truth-serum question, and
it should be followed by actually making the calls.

THE REFERENCE CALL IS NOT OPTIONAL. Ask the reference the same
question: "They told me you'd say X — how does that match your
view?" and then: "On a scale of 1-10, how would you rate their
performance? ... What would have made it a 10?" Nobody gives a
bad reference; everybody answers the "what would make it a 10"
question honestly.

SECTION 3: THE PSYCHOLOGY OF THE OWNER WHO WON'T HIRE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Most service business owners delay their first real hire by a year
or more. Not for lack of money. For these five reasons — and each
has a documented answer.

1. "NO ONE WILL DO IT AS WELL AS ME"
   True, on day one. Irrelevant, because the comparison is wrong.
   The hire is not competing with you at your best. They are
   competing with you at 9pm, tired, doing the task for the 40th
   time. The SOP plus the checklist plus the four-run ramp gets
   a reliable person to "good enough, consistently" — which beats
   "brilliant, occasionally, resentfully."

2. "I GOT BURNED LAST TIME"
   Almost always, the last hire failed because the role was
   undefined. The person was hired for a vibe, given no documented
   process, and blamed for the chaos that followed. That is a
   hiring-system failure, not a people failure. This week fixes
   the system.

3. "I CAN'T AFFORD IT"
   Last week's Rent Calculator answered this. The hours locked in
   your head have a dollar figure. A part-time operator costs a
   fraction of it. The math is usually not close.

4. "IT'LL TAKE LONGER TO TRAIN THEM THAN TO DO IT"
   True for one run. False for the 50th. The four-run ramp
   (watch → do with review → do with spot-check → own it) front-
   loads the cost into one week, on purpose.

5. "I DON'T KNOW HOW TO INTERVIEW"
   Correct — and neither does anyone who hasn't been taught.
   Structure replaces instinct. The interview kit this week is a
   script. Follow it.

SECTION 4: WHAT WORLD-CLASS SMALL-BUSINESS HIRERS DO DIFFERENTLY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

THEY HIRE FOR THE SYSTEM, NOT THE STORY
   The role is the bundle of SOPs. The ad says so. The interview
   tests whether the person can run a documented process and
   escalate correctly. Charm is not on the scorecard.

THEY WRITE THE AD AS A FILTER, NOT A PITCH
   A great ad makes the wrong candidates leave. "Not for you if..."
   is the most valuable section of the ad. The goal is fewer, better
   applications — not more.

THEY SCREEN WITH ONE HARD QUESTION
   Every application form includes a question that can only be
   answered by someone who read the ad carefully and can actually
   do the work. It filters out mass-appliers before you spend a
   minute.

THEY PAY FOR THE WORK SAMPLE
   A one-to-two hour paid task, drawn from a real SOP, before any
   final interview. It respects the candidate, it produces the
   most predictive data you will get, and it shows you how they
   handle a real document under real conditions.

THEY SCORE BEFORE THEY TALK
   Every interviewer fills in the rubric alone. Then they compare.
   The strongest first impression in the room does not get to set
   the verdict.

THEY START WITH A PROJECT, NOT A CONTRACT
   For contractors and part-time roles especially: a defined
   four-to-six-week paid trial, scoped to one SOP, with the
   scorecard outcomes made explicit. Both sides learn the truth
   fast, and the exit is clean if it's wrong.

THEY DECIDE ON THE SCORECARD, NOT THE GUT
   Gut gets a vote. It does not get a veto over a candidate who
   scored well across the board — and it does not get to rescue
   a candidate who scored poorly but "felt right."

SECTION 5: THE MOST COMMON HIRING FAILURES
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

FAILURE 1 — THE DUTIES LIST
   The ad is a list of tasks. Nobody can tell what success looks
   like, including the owner. FIX: outcomes, not duties.

FAILURE 2 — THE UNICORN
   One role that requires a designer, a copywriter, a project
   manager, and a bookkeeper. Nobody applies, or the wrong person
   does. FIX: bundle SOPs by skill family; one role, one family.

FAILURE 3 — THE FRIENDLY CHAT
   Forty-five minutes of unstructured conversation and a gut call.
   FIX: same questions, written rubric, independent scoring.

FAILURE 4 — SKIPPING THE WORK SAMPLE
   Hiring on talk. FIX: a paid task from a real SOP, every time.

FAILURE 5 — NO REFERENCE CALLS
   "They seemed great." FIX: the Topgrading question in the
   interview, then two calls minimum before any offer.

FAILURE 6 — HIRING A MINI-FOUNDER
   Looking for initiative and vision in a role that needs
   reliability and escalation judgment. The mismatch frustrates
   both people. FIX: match the competencies to the role's actual
   variance — low-variance roles want consistency; high-variance
   roles want judgment.

FAILURE 7 — NO 90-DAY STANDARD
   The hire starts, and nobody defined what "working out" means.
   FIX: the scorecard's outcomes are the 90-day review. Write
   them before the ad goes up.

SECTION 6: ANATOMY OF A HIRING AD THAT FILTERS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Seven parts, in this order:

1. THE TITLE — plain, searchable, honest. "Client Delivery
   Coordinator (part-time, remote)" beats "Operations Ninja."
2. THE ONE-PARAGRAPH TRUTH — what the business does, who it
   serves, and why this role exists now. Written like a person.
3. WHAT YOU'LL OWN — the outcomes from the scorecard, in plain
   language. 3-5 bullets. Results, not tasks.
4. WHAT A WEEK LOOKS LIKE — the actual processes (from the SOPs),
   the tools, the hours, the rhythm. Concrete enough to self-select.
5. THIS IS FOR YOU IF / NOT FOR YOU IF — 3-4 bullets each. The
   "not for you" list is the filter. Be honest about the parts
   that are repetitive, deadline-bound, or unglamorous.
6. THE PRACTICAL BLOCK — hours, pay range (stated — ranges get
   more and better applicants), contractor or employee, time zone,
   start date, trial structure.
7. HOW TO APPLY — including the ONE screening question that
   proves they read the ad and can do the work.

WHERE TO POST (for service-business operator roles):
   - Your own network and client base first (warm referrals
     convert at the highest rate and cost nothing)
   - Specialist VA / operator marketplaces for part-time remote
   - General job boards last — highest volume, lowest signal

SECTION 7: THE SELECTION FUNNEL
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Five stages. Each one removes people cheaply before the next
stage costs more.

STAGE 1 — APPLICATION SCREEN (2 minutes each)
   The screening question. Did they read the ad? Can they write
   clearly? Did they follow the instruction? Cut 60-80% here.

STAGE 2 — SCREENING CALL (15-20 minutes)
   Four questions: What are you looking for? What are you good at
   professionally? What are you not good at or not interested in?
   Who were your last managers and how will they rate you when we
   call? Cut half.

STAGE 3 — PAID WORK SAMPLE (1-2 hours, their time)
   A real task from a real SOP. Score it against the SOP's
   Definition of Done. This is the most predictive stage.
   Cut to your top 2-3.

STAGE 4 — STRUCTURED INTERVIEW (45-60 minutes)
   The chronological walk + behavioral questions per competency +
   Adler's accomplishment deep-dive. Scored on the rubric.

STAGE 5 — REFERENCE CALLS (15 minutes each, 2 minimum)
   Confirm what the candidate predicted the reference would say.
   Then the decision, on the scorecard.

TIME BUDGET FOR THE OWNER: roughly 8-12 hours total across the
funnel for a part-time operator role, if the stages are respected.
Skipping stages does not save time; it moves the time to the
three months after a bad hire.

SECTION 8: REAL-WORLD PATTERNS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

THE KAHNEMAN PATTERN — score attributes, then decide
Asked to fix the Israeli army's officer selection, Kahneman replaced
the unstructured "gut" interview with six independently rated
traits, scored in order, with the overall judgment deferred to the
end. Predictive validity rose substantially, and he later reported
that even the interviewers' final intuition became more accurate —
once it was formed AFTER the structured scores, not before. The
lesson: intuition is fine as the last input. It is disastrous as
the first.

THE WORK-SAMPLE PATTERN — let the SOP do the interviewing
An owner with a documented client-report process hands a finalist
the SOP, the checklist, a sample data set, and the time estimate.
The candidate either produces the report to the Definition of Done
in roughly the estimated time, or does not. Forty-five minutes of
conversation could not have revealed what that one task did.

THE FILTER-AD PATTERN — fewer applicants, better hire
An ad with an honest "not for you if" section and a real screening
question typically draws a fraction of the applications of a
generic posting — and a far higher proportion of them are worth a
call. Volume was never the goal.

THE TRIAL-PROJECT PATTERN — the contract writes itself
A four-week paid trial scoped to one SOP, with the scorecard
outcomes stated up front, converts to an ongoing role when it
works and ends cleanly when it doesn't. Both parties know the
standard from day one because the standard was written before
the ad.

SECTION 9: THE OPERATING PRINCIPLES CLAUDE WILL APPLY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

When working through Step 2 prompts, Claude will apply these
principles to every output, automatically:

PRINCIPLE 1: THE SOPs ARE THE ROLE
Every role is defined by bundling documented processes. No role
is defined from a wish list.

PRINCIPLE 2: SCORECARD BEFORE AD
No ad is written until the mission, outcomes, and competencies
exist. The ad is derived from the scorecard, never the reverse.

PRINCIPLE 3: OUTCOMES, NOT DUTIES
Every role element is expressed as a measurable result with a
timeframe. Activity lists are rewritten as results.

PRINCIPLE 4: THE AD IS A FILTER
Every ad carries an honest "not for you if" list and a screening
question that proves the applicant read it and can do the work.

PRINCIPLE 5: STRUCTURE OVER INSTINCT
Same questions, same order, written rubric, independent scoring.
Gut gets the last vote, never the first.

PRINCIPLE 6: A WORK SAMPLE, EVERY TIME
Every selection process includes a paid task drawn from a real
SOP, scored against its Definition of Done.

PRINCIPLE 7: REFERENCES ARE MANDATORY
The Topgrading prediction question in the interview, then the
calls before any offer. No exceptions.

PRINCIPLE 8: MATCH THE PERSON TO THE VARIANCE
Low-variance roles are hired for consistency and follow-through.
High-variance roles are hired for judgment and escalation instinct.
The competencies reflect which one this is.
