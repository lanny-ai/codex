SECTION 1: THE CORE TRUTH ABOUT TRAINING
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

The line that should be printed above every training document
ever written comes from a wartime manufacturing program, and it
has not been improved on since:

"IF THE WORKER HASN'T LEARNED, THE INSTRUCTOR HASN'T TAUGHT."
— Training Within Industry (TWI), U.S. War Manpower Commission, 1940s

Most small-business training is a one-way transfer: the owner
explains, the new person nods, and three days later the work comes
back wrong. The owner concludes the person "doesn't get it." TWI
concluded the opposite, and built the most successful training
method of the twentieth century on that conclusion. Toyota adopted
it wholesale; it became the training backbone of standardized work.

The second truth, from Cathy Moore, whose action mapping method
has reshaped corporate training design:

TRAINING IS NOT INFORMATION. TRAINING IS PRACTICE.

"What do people need to DO, and what's the minimum they need to
KNOW to do it?" Most training documents are information dumps —
everything the owner knows about the topic, organized by topic.
Almost none of it survives contact with the actual work. Moore's
method designs backward from the business result, through the
observable behaviors that produce it, to practice activities that
build those behaviors — and only THEN to the minimum information
needed to practice. Information is the last thing you add, not
the first.

The third truth, from a century of memory research:

PEOPLE FORGET MOST OF WHAT THEY'RE TOLD WITHIN DAYS —
UNLESS THEY RETRIEVE IT AND USE IT.

Ebbinghaus mapped the forgetting curve in the 1880s. Roediger and
Karpicke's testing-effect research showed that being made to
RETRIEVE information — practice, quizzes, doing the task — produces
far more durable learning than re-reading or re-hearing it. Spacing
that retrieval over days beats cramming. (Make It Stick, Brown,
Roediger & McDaniel.) A training doc someone reads once is nearly
worthless. A training doc that makes them do the thing, four times,
across a week, is a system.

What this means for the member: last week's SOPs are the REFERENCE.
Last week's scorecard is the STANDARD. Neither is training. This
week builds the thing that turns a competent stranger into someone
who can run the system — and proves it with a checkout, not a vibe.

SECTION 2: THE FOUR MASTER FRAMEWORKS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

FRAMEWORK 1 — TWI JOB INSTRUCTION (the four-step method)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The method that trained millions of new workers fast, with quality,
during a labor shortage — and the ancestor of Toyota's training.

STEP 1: PREPARE THE LEARNER
   Put them at ease. State the job. Find out what they already know.
   Get them interested. Put them in the correct position to see.

STEP 2: PRESENT THE OPERATION
   Tell, show, and illustrate ONE important step at a time.
   Stress each KEY POINT. Instruct clearly, completely, patiently —
   but no more than they can master. (Cognitive load is real.)

STEP 3: TRY OUT PERFORMANCE
   Have them do the job — correct errors as they go.
   Have them explain each key point TO YOU as they do it again.
   Make sure they understand. Continue until YOU know THEY know.

STEP 4: FOLLOW UP
   Put them on their own. Designate who to ask for help.
   Check frequently. Encourage questions. Taper off coaching.

Notice how precisely Step 4 matches the four-run ramp from Week 1.
That is not a coincidence — the ramp IS Job Instruction Follow Up.

THE JOB BREAKDOWN SHEET — TWI's teaching document, distinct from
the SOP. Three columns:
   IMPORTANT STEPS — what to do (a logical segment that advances the work)
   KEY POINTS      — how to do it: anything that makes or breaks the job,
                     injures the worker, or makes it easier
   REASONS         — why the key point matters
The SOP tells you the steps. The breakdown sheet tells you which
steps are tricky, what the trick is, and why. Training happens from
the breakdown sheet, with the SOP open as the reference.

FRAMEWORK 2 — ACTION MAPPING (Cathy Moore)
───────────────────────────────────────────

Design backward. Four layers, in this order:

   1. THE BUSINESS GOAL — measurable. For this role, it is the
      scorecard's outcomes. Already written.
   2. WHAT PEOPLE NEED TO DO — observable actions on the job that
      produce the goal. Not "understand the client process" —
      "produce the monthly report to the Definition of Done by the
      3rd business day."
   3. PRACTICE ACTIVITIES — realistic tasks that make them do #2,
      with the real tools, real inputs, and realistic ambiguity.
      The activity IS the training.
   4. THE MINIMUM INFORMATION — only what's needed to complete the
      practice. Everything else is reference material, linked, not
      taught.

THE TEST: for every piece of content in a training doc, ask "which
practice activity does this support?" If the answer is none, it is
reference, not training — move it to the SOP or cut it.

WHY THIS MATTERS: Owners over-explain because explaining feels like
teaching. Action mapping replaces "here's everything about X" with
"here are four things you'll do this week, and here's exactly what
you need to do them."

FRAMEWORK 3 — RETRIEVAL, SPACING, AND CHECKOUTS (the learning science)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

THE TESTING EFFECT: retrieving knowledge strengthens it far more
than reviewing it. A new hire who runs the process from the
checklist, closes the doc, and explains the steps back is learning.
One who reads the SOP twice is not.

SPACING: four practice runs across four days beat four runs in one
afternoon. The four-run ramp already spaces; protect that spacing.

INTERLEAVING: once two or three processes are in play, mixing them
across the week (report, then onboarding, then report) builds more
robust skill than blocking (all reports, then all onboardings).

THE COMPETENCE CHECKOUT — the airline and medical model: nobody is
signed off on a procedure because they attended training. They are
signed off because they DEMONSTRATED it, observed, against written
pass criteria. For a service business:
   - The criteria are the SOP's Definition of Done plus the
     checklist's killer items
   - The observer watches a real or realistic run
   - Pass / not yet — with the specific gap named
   - The sign-off is dated and recorded
A checkout converts "I think they've got it" into a fact.

THE FORGETTING SCHEDULE: anything not used for three or more weeks
needs a refresher before the next real run. Rare processes get a
"before you run this" review step built into the SOP itself.

FRAMEWORK 4 — COGNITIVE LOAD AND THE 70-20-10 REALITY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────

COGNITIVE LOAD (Sweller): working memory holds a handful of new
things at once. Training that presents twelve steps, six tools, and
four exceptions in one sitting produces nothing but nodding.
   - Chunk: one SOP phase at a time
   - Worked examples first: show a completed, correct output before
     asking for one
   - Strip anything that is not needed for THIS practice task
   - Sequence simple → complex: the low-variance checklist process
     before the high-variance decision-tree one

70-20-10 (McCall, Lombardo & Eichinger's Center for Creative
Leadership research): people report roughly 70% of their
development comes from doing the work, 20% from other people
(feedback, coaching, examples), 10% from formal instruction. The
exact numbers are debated; the ordering is not. Design the
training so that most of the hours are DOING with feedback, and
the documents exist to make the doing possible.

THE IMPLICATION: A "training doc" is mostly a practice plan with
a reference attached — not a textbook.

SECTION 3: THE PSYCHOLOGY OF WHY TRAINING FAILS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

1. THE OWNER TEACHES THE WAY THEY LEARNED — which was by doing it
   200 times, alone. They have no memory of being new. The breakdown
   sheet's KEY POINTS column exists to force the expert to name the
   tricky parts they no longer notice.

2. EXPLAINING FEELS PRODUCTIVE. Talking for an hour feels like
   training. It transfers almost nothing. Action mapping is the
   corrective: what will they DO in the next hour?

3. THE NEW PERSON WON'T SAY "I DON'T UNDERSTAND." Ever. Not on day
   two. TWI's Step 3 — have them explain the key points back while
   doing it — is the only reliable detector.

4. QUESTIONS GET ANSWERED AND NOT CAPTURED. The first hire asks
   forty questions in week one. If each answer goes into the doc,
   the second hire asks ten. If not, the second hire asks forty.
   The "question asked → doc updated" rule is the highest-return
   habit in this entire month.

5. THE HANDOFF HAS NO END CONDITION. "I'll check their work for a
   while" becomes forever. The checkout is the end condition: pass,
   dated, signed — and then the owner stops reviewing every output.

SECTION 4: WHAT WORLD-CLASS SMALL-BUSINESS TRAINERS DO DIFFERENTLY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

THEY TRAIN FROM THE BREAKDOWN, REFERENCE FROM THE SOP
   Two documents, two jobs. The breakdown sheet is short and names
   the tricky parts. The SOP is complete and lives beside it.

THEY RECORD ONCE, TEACH FOREVER
   The 📹 shot list from Week 1 becomes the Present step. A 4-minute
   screen recording of the owner running the phase — talking through
   the key points — replaces the live walkthrough for every future
   hire. Record the key points out loud, not just the clicks.

THEY SHOW THE FINISHED THING FIRST
   Before the new person produces a report, they see a correct one,
   annotated: "this is what done looks like, and here's why each
   part matters." Worked examples cut errors more than any
   instruction.

THEY MAKE THE FIRST RUN SAFE
   A practice run on a sample client, a sandbox, or last month's
   real data — never a live client on day one. The practice inputs
   are part of the training kit.

THEY PLANT THE EXCEPTION
   Every practice run includes one realistic thing the document
   doesn't cover, to rehearse the escalation path before it matters.
   Rehearsed escalation is fast; unrehearsed escalation is a guess.

THEY CHECK OUT, THEN GET OUT
   A dated, criteria-based sign-off, and then the owner stops
   reviewing every output. The checkout exists so the owner can
   actually let go.

THEY TREAT THE FAQ AS A LIVING DOCUMENT
   Every question the new person asks is a hole in the docs. The
   answer goes into the FAQ or the SOP the same day. By hire three,
   onboarding is mostly self-serve.

SECTION 5: THE MOST COMMON TRAINING DOC FAILURES
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

FAILURE 1 — THE TEXTBOOK
   Forty pages about the business, the philosophy, the tools, and
   the history. Nobody finishes it. FIX: action mapping — practice
   tasks with the minimum information attached.

FAILURE 2 — THE SOP RE-USED AS TRAINING
   Handing someone the reference document and calling it training.
   FIX: the job breakdown sheet + Present/Try Out/Follow Up.

FAILURE 3 — NO PRACTICE INPUTS
   "Just try it on the next real client." FIX: sample data, a
   sandbox account, or last month's real inputs, packaged.

FAILURE 4 — NO CHECKOUT
   Training "ends" when the owner gets tired of checking. FIX:
   written pass criteria, an observed run, a dated sign-off.

FAILURE 5 — ALL AT ONCE
   Every process, every tool, in week one. FIX: sequence by the
   ramp — one process to checkout before the next starts, simple
   before complex.

FAILURE 6 — THE UNANSWERED "WHY"
   Steps with no reasons get skipped as soon as they're
   inconvenient. FIX: the REASONS column on every key point.

FAILURE 7 — QUESTIONS EVAPORATE
   Answered in Slack, never captured. FIX: the question → doc rule,
   with an owner and a same-day standard.

SECTION 6: ANATOMY OF A TRAINING MODULE THAT WORKS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

One module per SOP. Eight parts:

1. WHAT YOU'LL BE ABLE TO DO — one sentence, observable, tied to
   the scorecard outcome this process serves.
2. WHAT DONE LOOKS LIKE — the worked example: a real, correct,
   finished output, annotated.
3. THE JOB BREAKDOWN — important steps / key points / reasons.
   Short. The SOP is linked, not repeated.
4. WATCH — the 📹 recording(s) for this process, with what to
   listen for.
5. PRACTICE — the try-out task: sample inputs, the checklist, the
   time target, and the one planted exception. Instructions for
   explaining the key points back.
6. THE CHECKOUT — pass criteria (from the Definition of Done and
   the killer items), who observes, how it's recorded.
7. WHEN IT GOES SIDEWAYS — the exception table from the SOP, plus
   the escalation rule, restated for the learner.
8. AFTER SIGN-OFF — the ramp stage they're at, what changes, and
   the refresher rule if this process is rare.

SECTION 7: THE 30-DAY ONBOARDING ARC
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WEEK 1 — ORIENT + FIRST PROCESS
   Day 1: access, context (the Business File's §1-§3 give a new
   person the business, the client, and the offer in ten minutes),
   the handbook, the first module's WATCH step.
   Days 2-5: first process on the four-run ramp. Nothing else.
   Friday: first check-in, questions captured.

WEEK 2 — CHECKOUT + SECOND PROCESS
   Checkout on process one. Begin process two. Interleave: a run of
   process one continues while two is learned.

WEEK 3 — THIRD PROCESS + INDEPENDENCE ON ONE AND TWO
   Owner stops reviewing every output on checked-out processes.
   Spot-checks only. Third process starts its ramp.

WEEK 4 — FULL BUNDLE + THE 30-DAY REVIEW
   All processes in the role running. The 30-day check-in from the
   Hiring Kit: what's true, what's not, and the decision available.

SEQUENCING RULE: the lowest-variance process first. Checklist-led
processes build confidence and vocabulary; decision-tree processes
need that foundation.

SECTION 8: REAL-WORLD PATTERNS
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

THE TWI PATTERN — speed without sacrifice
Wartime plants had to turn new workers into skilled ones in weeks,
not years. Job Instruction did it by refusing to separate teaching
from doing: present a step, have them do it, have them explain it
back, repeat. The method survived because it works for anyone
teaching anyone a procedure — including an owner teaching a VA.

THE AIRLINE PATTERN — sign-off or nothing
A pilot with a thousand hours is still checked out on each new
aircraft type against written criteria by an observer. Experience
does not exempt anyone from demonstration. For a service business
this means: even a hire with a great résumé demonstrates YOUR
process to YOUR standard before running it alone.

THE FAQ PATTERN — the second hire is cheap
An owner who captures every week-one question into the docs finds
the second hire needs a fraction of the hand-holding. The first
onboarding is expensive; the documentation it produces makes every
subsequent one cheaper. That is the compounding this month is for.

THE WORKED-EXAMPLE PATTERN — show the destination
Cognitive-load research consistently finds that novices learn
faster from studying correct, annotated examples than from solving
problems cold. A folder of "here's what a perfect one looks like"
outputs is training material, and it costs the owner nothing but
the annotation.

SECTION 9: THE OPERATING PRINCIPLES CLAUDE WILL APPLY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

When working through Step 2 prompts, Claude will apply these
principles to every output, automatically:

PRINCIPLE 1: DESIGN BACKWARD FROM THE SCORECARD
Every module starts from a scorecard outcome and the observable
actions that produce it. Information comes last.

PRINCIPLE 2: PRACTICE IS THE TRAINING
Every module is built around a try-out task with real tools and
inputs. Content that supports no practice is reference, not
training — link it, don't teach it.

PRINCIPLE 3: TWO DOCUMENTS, TWO JOBS
The job breakdown sheet teaches; the SOP references. Never merge
them; never hand the SOP over as training.

PRINCIPLE 4: KEY POINTS AND REASONS, ALWAYS
Every important step names what makes or breaks it and why.

PRINCIPLE 5: EXPLAIN IT BACK
Every try-out includes the learner narrating the key points while
doing the work. It is the only reliable understanding check.

PRINCIPLE 6: CHECKOUT BEFORE INDEPENDENCE
No process is handed over without an observed run against written
criteria and a dated sign-off. The checkout is the end condition
for owner review.

PRINCIPLE 7: PLANT THE EXCEPTION
Every practice run includes one realistic case the document
doesn't cover, to rehearse escalation.

PRINCIPLE 8: ONE PROCESS AT A TIME, SIMPLE FIRST
Sequence by variance, low to high. Chunk by SOP phase. Respect
cognitive load.

PRINCIPLE 9: EVERY QUESTION UPDATES A DOCUMENT
Questions are documentation holes. The answer goes into the FAQ
or the SOP the same day.
