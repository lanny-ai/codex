# What the evidence says

Each claim carries its kind. Say which kind when you use one with a member.
- **founder's method**: what the founder does and teaches, from his own recordings and notes.
- **launch practice**: what several teachers agree on, from the research behind this skill.
- **data**: a published benchmark or a regulator's text; the source and its sample are named.
- **practitioner**: one teacher's claim about their own results, unverified.
- **folklore**: widely repeated, no traceable source. Never presented as fact.

## 1. Live first, then an honest recording

- The founder teaches that live interaction is what sells and that automated webinars are dead. [founder's method]
- Teachers who disagree on almost everything else agree that a recording is never presented as live; one middle path is to find the winning talk live, then automate the winner. [launch practice: Frank Kern, Michael Stevenson]
- So the skill builds live first, and offers a recorded version only after a live run has converted (`evergreen.md`).

## 2. Show-up: two figures, never one

- Live attendance runs about a third to a half of registrants: 47.7% across 33,786 sessions in 2025 [data: Livestorm 2026 report]; 33% average attendance [data: Goldcast 2025, B2B]; about 49% live [data: Univid via Zoom's blog].
- Counting people who watch the replay, about 57–60% of registrants watch some of it, and on-demand viewers are about half of all viewers [data: ON24 2025 and 2026 reports, enterprise B2B, so high for a small business].
- A reminder 1 hour before the start doubled live attendance against one sent at the start time (50.7% vs 24.6%) [data: WebinarGeek benchmark, 2024 data]. Reminders are the Event Launcher's job; point there.
- No published benchmark exists for small-business or coaching sales webinars. Plan on "about a third to a half live" as a starting point and count your own.
- A talk does not fix show-up. "The fix lives in top-of-funnel, not in the webinar itself" was the founder's diagnosis in one strategy report. [founder's method]

## 3. Length: 60 minutes, ending on time

- The experts disagree: 2 hours 15 minutes [practitioner: Stevenson], 2–3 hours for a desperate audience [practitioner: Jason Fladlien, 2018], about 50 minutes to the close [practitioner: Mike Koenigs], 40–45 minutes [2025 mastermind advice].
- Average watch time is 29 minutes [data: Goldcast 2025] to 49 minutes of engagement [data: ON24]; attendees in one survey preferred 30–45 minutes [data, weak: WorkCast via Zoom].
- The founder says he would not sit through his own 4-hour days, and attendees agree. [founder's method]
- The skill's choice for this audience: 60 minutes, the strongest teaching before minute 30, the offer starting by about minute 40, Q&A to minute 60, and a hard stop. A member who wants longer runs a bootcamp (`talk-structures.md` §3) with the Event Launcher.

## 4. The teaching sells by closing its loops

- Each teaching block confirms what the viewer suspects, admits a fault of the member's own industry, is self-evident, and answers its own question; a block that leaves questions open makes people believe they can finish it alone, so they do not buy. [practitioner: Stevenson]
- Teach the first step, not the expert's whole system; education is measured by what people do differently afterwards. [practitioner: Fladlien]
- Three blocks, because people can keep track of three. [practitioner: Koenigs; launch practice]
- "Demonstrate you can help by actually helping." [practitioner: Kern]
- When a day fell flat, the diagnosis was that it never created a gap between where people were and where they could be. [founder's method]

## 5. Say there will be an offer; name the objections early

- Announce both halves up front: something useful today, and an offer at the end. [launch practice: a stage-close script in the founder's archive; Kern's invite emails disclose the last minutes are the offer]
- Name the top objections as "what we are not covering today", each answered in the act of setting it aside. [launch practice]
- "An objection is a question you didn't answer": list the recurring ones and answer each earlier in the talk. [practitioner]
- Script the Q&A for time, money, confidence and the spouse. [practitioner: Stevenson]
- "This may or may not be for you" removes the tension. [founder's method]

## 6. The offer

- One core offer per webinar, with no more than three doors. Prices and terms come only from `close.md`. [launch practice; founder's failure list: inconsistent prices quoted across days]
- Ask three times, with the link on screen and in the chat. [practitioner: Mitch Carson]
- A "what's on offer" summary people can download, because an attendee asked for one when offers came in and out. [founder's method]
- Most buyers buy after the event, not during it; the follow-up carries the sale. [founder's method] Plan the talk to make the next step obvious, not to force a decision on air.
- Do not talk past the close: go straight to the link. [founder's method]

## 7. Review every run

- Record everything and ask AI where it went wrong and how to recover. [founder's method]
- Rescripting only the first five minutes of a long presentation lifted close rate by 20% at one company. [practitioner, secondhand]
- Fix in order: show-up, then stayed to the offer, then bought (or booked). Measure each; measuring is itself the intervention. [practitioner; launch practice]
- Judge a webinar on revenue after the follow-up window, not only during the session. [practitioner]

## 8. What the vault does not cover

Nothing in the research addresses an audience aged 55 to 75 directly. The choices in `older-audience.md` are the skill's own, built on WCAG 2.2 and the W3C's notes on older users, and labelled that way.
